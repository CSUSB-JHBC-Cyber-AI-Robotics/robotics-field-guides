The ROS 2 Field Guide

Nodes, Topics & the Robot Nervous System
A robot is not one program. It is dozens of small programs — one reading a camera, one estimating where the body is, one deciding where to step, one driving motors — all running at once and constantly telling each other what they know. ROS 2 is the plumbing that lets them do that without any of them knowing the others exist. This book teaches the publish–subscribe model at the centre of it, the command-line tools that let you eavesdrop on a live robot, and enough of the DDS layer underneath to debug the day it stops working.
A companion in the Field Guide series · pairs with the Unitree G1 & Networking guides · offline, self-contained

How to Read This Book — Front Matter

ROS 2 has a reputation for a steep learning curve, and it's half deserved: the ideas are genuinely simple, but the tooling around them is large and the documentation assumes you already know the shape of the thing. This book front-loads the shape.

The journey, part by part Part I (The Model) is the conceptual core and the part that transfers everywhere — nodes, the graph, topics, messages, services, actions. Read it properly; everything else is mechanics. Part II (Working with a Robot) is the hands-on half: installing, the command-line tools that let you inspect a live system, writing your first nodes in Python, building a workspace with colcon, and launch files. Part III (The Layer Underneath) is what separates people who can use ROS 2 from people who can fix it — DDS and QoS, coordinate frames, and the visualization and recording tools. Part IV is troubleshooting, labs, and reference.
How to actually use it You do not need a robot. ROS 2 installs on any Ubuntu machine and ships with demo nodes, and nearly every exercise in this book runs entirely on a laptop — which is the right way to learn, because a robot adds hardware failures to your software confusion. Open two or three terminals and keep them open; multi-terminal work is simply how ROS 2 is used. If you're coming to this from the Unitree G1 guide, note that the G1's own SDK speaks DDS directly rather than going through ROS 2 — Chapter 10 explains how those two worlds relate, and it's worth reading early.
ROS 1 answers will mislead you There is a decade of ROS 1 material online and it ranks well in search results. ROS 2 is a ground-up rewrite: there is no roscore, catkin_make is now colcon build, rosrun is ros2 run, and the whole networking layer was replaced with DDS. If a tutorial mentions roscore or catkin, it is for ROS 1 and following it will waste your afternoon. Check that anything you read names a ROS 2 distribution — Humble, Jazzy, Kilted — before you trust it.

Part I — The Model

What ROS 2 Is — Chapter 1

The name is misleading in a way that's worth clearing up in the first paragraph: ROS is not an operating system, and understanding what it actually is makes the rest of the system much easier to reason about.

Middleware, plus a toolbox, plus conventions

ROS 2 is three things stacked together:

  • Middleware — a way for separate programs to send each other structured messages without knowing anything about each other. This is the heart of it.
  • A toolbox — command-line tools to inspect a running system, a build system, a visualizer, a recorder, a coordinate-frame library.
  • Conventions — agreed message formats and topic names, so a LIDAR driver from one vendor and a mapping package from another can be connected without either author having met.

That third point is the real reason ROS won. When every robot's camera publishes sensor_msgs/msg/Image, any package that consumes images works with any camera. The ecosystem effect — the thousands of existing packages for navigation, manipulation, perception, simulation — is what you're really adopting.

The problem it solves

Write a robot as one big program and you get something that cannot be debugged, cannot be reused, and dies entirely when any part of it crashes. Split it into separate processes and you inherit a new problem: they need to talk, across processes and often across machines, at high rates, without blocking each other. Solving that well — serialization, discovery, transport, reliability, timing — is months of work you'd rather not repeat per project. ROS 2 is that work, done once.

Distributions

ROS 2 ships as named distributions, each tied to an Ubuntu release. The pairing is strict, and choosing wrong is the single most common way to lose a first day:

DistributionReleasedEOLTier 1 UbuntuStatus
Jazzy JaliscoMay 2024May 202924.04 (Noble)LTS — the safe default today
Lyrical LuthMay 2026May 203126.04LTS, newest; ecosystem still catching up
Kilted KaijuMay 2025Nov 202624.04 (Noble)Non-LTS, ~1.5 year window
Humble HawksbillMay 2022May 202722.04 (Jammy)LTS, older; still very widely deployed

New distributions land every year on 23 May (World Turtle Day); LTS releases get five years of support, non-LTS about eighteen months. Note that Jazzy and Kilted share Ubuntu 24.04 — a distribution has full Tier 1 support for exactly one Ubuntu LTS, but one Ubuntu release can host more than one distribution.

Pick the LTS that matches your Ubuntu, and match whatever your lab or the package you depend on uses. As of this writing Jazzy is the conservative choice: deepest package ecosystem, four more years of support. Lyrical Luth is newer and also LTS, but third-party packages lag a fresh release by months. Mixing distributions on one machine is possible but not a thing to attempt while learning.

Nodes & the Graph — Chapter 2

A node is a program that does one job. The graph is every node currently running, plus every connection between them. That is the whole architecture, and it's worth holding in your head as a literal picture.

Nodes

A node is a single process with a name, doing one thing: /camera_driver, /face_detector, /joint_controller. The design principle is one responsibility per node, and it buys you real things — you can restart one node without touching the rest, run it on a different machine by changing nothing, swap a simulated driver for a real one, and test each piece alone.

The graph

Decoupling is the point

Nodes never address each other. /camera publishes to /image_raw and does not know or care who is listening — zero subscribers, or five. /perception subscribes to /image_raw and does not know what is producing it. This decoupling is what makes the whole thing work:

  • Replace the real camera with a simulator or a recorded log — nothing downstream changes.
  • Add a recorder or a visualizer to any topic at runtime, without modifying a line of anyone's code.
  • Move a node to another machine; it finds its peers automatically (Chapter 10).
  • Debug by inserting yourself into the graph as just another subscriber — which is exactly what ros2 topic echo is.

No master

ROS 1 had roscore, a central registry every node connected through — and a single point of failure. ROS 2 has no master. Nodes discover each other by broadcasting on the network via DDS (Chapter 10). Start nodes in any order; they'll find each other when they appear. This is a genuine improvement and also the source of ROS 2's most confusing failure mode: when discovery silently doesn't happen, everything looks fine and nothing connects.

Topics & Messages — Chapter 3

Topics carry the great majority of robot data. If you understand publish–subscribe you understand most of ROS 2 — and, as it happens, most of modern robotics middleware generally.

Publish and subscribe

A topic is a named channel carrying one type of message. Nodes publish to it or subscribe to it. It's a broadcast: every subscriber receives every message, and the publisher is not blocked or slowed by them.

The mental model that helps most is a radio station. /scan is a frequency. The LIDAR broadcasts on it continuously whether or not anyone is tuned in. Anyone who wants the data tunes in. Nobody requests anything, and nobody waits.

Message types

Every topic has exactly one type, defined in a .msg file and shared by all parties. A type mismatch means no connection — and, unhelpfully, no error either. Types are namespaced by package:

std_msgs/msg/String            # a string
sensor_msgs/msg/Image          # a camera frame
sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan      # a 2D laser sweep
sensor_msgs/msg/JointState     # joint names, positions, velocities, efforts
geometry_msgs/msg/Twist        # linear + angular velocity — the classic drive command
nav_msgs/msg/Odometry          # estimated pose and velocity

Appendix B lists the ones you'll actually meet. Inspect any of them with ros2 interface show.

Headers, timestamps, and frames

Most sensor messages begin with a std_msgs/msg/Header carrying a timestamp and a frame_id. Both matter enormously and both are easy to get wrong. The timestamp is what lets a node fuse a camera frame with a LIDAR sweep taken 12 ms apart rather than naively assuming they're simultaneous. The frame_id says which coordinate frame the data is expressed in — "3.2 metres away" is meaningless without knowing away from what (Chapter 11).

Try it — watch a graph from the outside 
# Terminal 1 — a talker node that ships with ROS 2
ros2 run demo_nodes_cpp talker

# Terminal 2 — a listener
ros2 run demo_nodes_py listener

# Terminal 3 — inspect the live system without touching either
ros2 node list
ros2 topic list
ros2 topic echo /chatter            # you are now a third subscriber
ros2 topic info /chatter --verbose  # who publishes, who subscribes, what QoS
ros2 topic hz /chatter              # actual publish rate
ros2 topic pub /chatter std_msgs/msg/String "{data: 'injected'}" --once
That last command matters: you published into a running system from the shell, and the listener received it identically to the real talker's messages. Nothing in the graph could tell the difference — that's decoupling made concrete.
From the field — this pattern is everywhere Publish–subscribe is not a ROS idea, and recognizing it elsewhere is one of the more portable things you'll take from this book. The Unitree G1 uses it over raw DDS with no ROS involved (G1 guide). Self-driving stacks, industrial control over MQTT, financial market data feeds, air-traffic systems, and essentially every event-driven web backend all use the same shape: named channels, decoupled producers and consumers, broadcast delivery. Learn to think in topics once — who publishes what, at what rate, and who cares — and you can walk up to an unfamiliar distributed system of almost any kind and start asking productive questions immediately. That transferability is a large part of why this chapter is worth reading twice.

Services & Actions — Chapter 4

Topics are a firehose with no acknowledgement, which is wrong for two common situations: asking a question and waiting for the answer, and starting a long job you want to monitor. Services and actions cover those.

Services — request and reply

A service is a function call across the network: one client, one server, one request, one response. Use it for things that are quick and need an answer — "reset the odometry", "what's the battery level", "switch to position mode".

ros2 service list
ros2 service type /reset
ros2 service call /reset std_srvs/srv/Empty
ros2 interface show std_srvs/srv/SetBool

The critical caveat: a service call blocks until the server responds. Calling a slow service from inside a control loop will stall that loop, and calling a service from within a callback can deadlock the node outright — a classic beginner trap that produces a node which simply freezes with no error.

Actions — long jobs with feedback

An action is for work that takes time and that you want to watch or abort: "walk to the kitchen", "pick up that cup". It combines three things — a goal you send, periodic feedback while it runs, and a final result — plus the ability to cancel.

ros2 action list
ros2 action info /navigate_to_pose -t
ros2 action send_goal /fibonacci example_interfaces/action/Fibonacci "{order: 8}" --feedback

Choosing between the three

UseWhenExample
TopicContinuous data; many listeners; nobody needs a replyCamera frames, joint states, velocity commands
ServiceQuick question, need the answer, happens occasionallyGet a parameter, reset a counter, toggle a mode
ActionTakes seconds or minutes; want progress; might cancelNavigate somewhere, execute a trajectory, calibrate

The rule of thumb that keeps people out of trouble: if in doubt, use a topic. Topics are non-blocking, easy to inspect from the command line, and easy to record and replay. Reach for a service only when you genuinely need a reply, and for an action only when the job is long enough that you'd want to watch it.

Part II — Working with a Robot

Installing ROS 2 — Chapter 5

Straightforward if your Ubuntu version matches your chosen distribution, and painful if it doesn't. Check that first.

Install Jazzy on Ubuntu 24.04

# Locale — ROS 2 needs a UTF-8 locale
sudo apt update && sudo apt install -y locales
sudo locale-gen en_US en_US.UTF-8
sudo update-locale LC_ALL=en_US.UTF-8 LANG=en_US.UTF-8
export LANG=en_US.UTF-8

# Enable the universe repository
sudo apt install -y software-properties-common curl
sudo add-apt-repository universe

# Add the ROS 2 apt source. This is the CURRENT method: a small .deb that
# configures the repository and its signing key for you. Older guides tell you
# to curl ros.key into /usr/share/keyrings by hand — that approach is superseded.
export ROS_APT_SOURCE_VERSION=$(curl -s \
  https://api.github.com/repos/ros-infrastructure/ros-apt-source/releases/latest \
  | grep -F "tag_name" | awk -F'"' '{print $4}')
curl -L -o /tmp/ros2-apt-source.deb \
  "https://github.com/ros-infrastructure/ros-apt-source/releases/download/${ROS_APT_SOURCE_VERSION}/ros2-apt-source_${ROS_APT_SOURCE_VERSION}.$(. /etc/os-release && echo ${UBUNTU_CODENAME:-${VERSION_CODENAME}})_all.deb"
sudo dpkg -i /tmp/ros2-apt-source.deb

# Install — desktop includes rviz2 and the demo nodes
sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y
sudo apt install -y ros-jazzy-desktop ros-dev-tools

Sourcing — the step everyone forgets

ROS 2 is not on your PATH until you source its setup script, in every new terminal:

source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash
ros2 --help                        # now it exists

# Verify the install with the demo pair:
ros2 run demo_nodes_cpp talker     # terminal 1
ros2 run demo_nodes_py listener    # terminal 2

You can add the source line to ~/.bashrc to automate it. Convenient while learning; a nuisance later if you work with multiple distributions or want a clean shell, since it silently affects every terminal you open. A common middle ground is a short alias:

echo "alias sj='source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash'" >> ~/.bashrc
Sourcing order matters with virtual environments ROS 2's Python packages are installed system-wide by apt, built against the exact Python that ships with your Ubuntu release. A normal isolated virtual environment cannot see them, and import rclpy will fail inside one. The fix is to source ROS first, then create the venv with --system-site-packages, then activate it — in that order. The Python Environment Field Guide's robotics chapter covers exactly this, and it is worth reading before you fight it for an hour.

The CLI Toolkit — Chapter 6

This is the chapter that makes you effective. The ros2 command lets you inspect, poke, and record a running robot without writing or modifying any code, and fluency here is most of what "being good at ROS 2" looks like day to day.

The shape of the command

Everything follows ros2 <noun> <verb>, which makes it guessable:

ros2 node list / info
ros2 topic list / echo / info / hz / pub / bw
ros2 service list / call / type
ros2 action list / send_goal
ros2 param list / get / set
ros2 interface show / list
ros2 bag record / play / info
ros2 run <package> <executable>
ros2 launch <package> <launch_file>
ros2 doctor

The ones you'll use constantly

ros2 topic list -t                       # topics WITH their types
ros2 topic echo /joint_states            # dump live messages
ros2 topic echo /scan --once             # exactly one, then stop
ros2 topic hz /image_raw                 # is it publishing at the rate it claims?
ros2 topic bw /image_raw                 # bandwidth — often surprisingly large
ros2 topic info /cmd_vel --verbose       # publishers, subscribers, and QoS
ros2 node info /perception               # everything one node is connected to
ros2 interface show sensor_msgs/msg/JointState

The debugging sequence

When something isn't working, this order finds the fault fast, and it's worth running through it before forming any theory:

  1. ros2 node list — is the node even running?
  2. ros2 topic list — does the topic exist? Check the exact name; /cmd_vel and /robot/cmd_vel are different topics and nothing will warn you.
  3. ros2 topic info <topic> --verbose — is anyone actually publishing, and does the subscriber count include yours?
  4. ros2 topic hz <topic> — is data flowing, and at the rate you expect?
  5. ros2 topic echo <topic> — is the content sane, or is it all zeros?

Ninety percent of the time you find it in the first three steps: a typo'd topic name, a node that died silently, or a QoS mismatch (Chapter 10).

From the field — echo before you theorize The most common failure mode in robotics debugging is not a hard problem — it's reasoning confidently about a system without having looked at it. Someone is sure the controller is wrong, and the controller is fine; the sensor has been publishing zeros for ten minutes because a cable is loose. ros2 topic echo costs three seconds and replaces the assumption with a fact. Build the habit of checking the data at each stage of a pipeline before forming a theory about which stage is broken, and you'll find that most "hard bugs" turn out to be somewhere you never would have looked, precisely because you were sure it wasn't there. This is the same instinct as checking sys.executable before debugging a Python import — look at reality first, cheaply, then think.

Writing Nodes in Python — Chapter 7

Enough Python to write a real node. The library is rclpy, and the structure is the same every time — which means once you've written one, you've essentially written all of them.

A publisher

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from std_msgs.msg import String


class Talker(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('talker')                       # the node's name
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(String, 'chatter', 10)
        self.timer = self.create_timer(0.5, self.tick)   # 2 Hz
        self.count = 0

    def tick(self):
        msg = String()
        msg.data = f'hello {self.count}'
        self.pub.publish(msg)
        self.get_logger().info(f'published: {msg.data}')
        self.count += 1


def main():
    rclpy.init()
    node = Talker()
    try:
        rclpy.spin(node)          # hand control to ROS; callbacks now fire
    except KeyboardInterrupt:
        pass
    finally:
        node.destroy_node()
        rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

A subscriber

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from sensor_msgs.msg import JointState


class JointWatcher(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('joint_watcher')
        self.sub = self.create_subscription(
            JointState, 'joint_states', self.on_joints, 10)

    def on_joints(self, msg):                 # called for every message
        if not msg.position:
            return
        hottest = max(range(len(msg.position)), key=lambda i: abs(msg.position[i]))
        self.get_logger().info(
            f'{len(msg.position)} joints, largest angle at index {hottest}')

The four things to understand

ThingWhat it means
rclpy.init()Starts the client library. Once per process, before anything else.
rclpy.spin(node)Hands control to ROS. Blocks forever, running callbacks as messages arrive.
CallbackYour function, called by ROS when a message arrives. You never call it yourself.
Queue depth (the 10)How many messages buffer if you can't keep up. Small for live sensor data — you want the newest, not a backlog.
Never block inside a callback By default a node runs callbacks one at a time on a single thread. A time.sleep(), a slow file write, a blocking service call, or a long computation inside a callback stops the entire node — every other subscription silently stops being serviced, messages pile up and get dropped, and there is no error message anywhere. The symptom is a node that "gets slow" or "misses messages" for no visible reason, and it is one of the most common real bugs in ROS 2 code. Keep callbacks short: copy the data out, set a flag, and do the real work in a timer or a separate thread. If you genuinely need concurrency, look at callback groups and the multi-threaded executor.

Workspaces & colcon — Chapter 8

A workspace is a folder of packages you build together. colcon is the tool that builds it. Two rules and one gotcha cover almost everything.

The layout

ros2_ws/
  src/                    <- your packages live here. Only this is yours.
    my_robot_pkg/
      package.xml         <- metadata and dependencies
      setup.py            <- Python build config (or CMakeLists.txt for C++)
      my_robot_pkg/
        __init__.py
        my_node.py
  build/                  <- generated. Safe to delete.
  install/                <- generated. Contains the setup script you source.
  log/                    <- generated. Safe to delete.

Creating and building

mkdir -p ~/ros2_ws/src && cd ~/ros2_ws/src
ros2 pkg create --build-type ament_python --node-name my_node my_robot_pkg

cd ~/ros2_ws
colcon build                              # build everything
colcon build --packages-select my_robot_pkg   # just one — much faster
colcon build --symlink-install            # Python edits apply without rebuilding

source install/setup.bash                 # <- the step everyone forgets
ros2 run my_robot_pkg my_node

Underlay and overlay

This is the concept worth understanding rather than memorizing. Your ROS installation at /opt/ros/jazzy is the underlay. Your workspace is an overlay on top of it, and its packages take precedence. So the order is always: source the underlay, build, source the overlay.

source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash     # underlay first
cd ~/ros2_ws && colcon build
source install/setup.bash            # overlay second — now yours wins

Get the order backwards and you'll run the installed version of a package while editing a local copy and wondering why nothing you change has any effect.

Never run colcon build from inside src/ colcon build must run from the workspace root — the folder containing src. Run it inside src/ and colcon creates a nested build/, install/, and log/ in there, producing a workspace-within-a-workspace whose symptoms are genuinely baffling: builds that succeed while your changes never take effect, packages found twice, stale code running. If it happens, delete the stray build/ install/ log/ from inside src/ and rebuild from the root. And when a build misbehaves in a way you can't explain, rm -rf build install log from the workspace root followed by a clean rebuild is a legitimate and common first move.

Launch Files & Parameters — Chapter 9

Real robots run twenty nodes. Starting them by hand in twenty terminals is not a plan. Launch files start a whole system with one command; parameters let you configure it without editing code.

A launch file

Launch files are Python (or XML/YAML) and live in a launch/ folder:

from launch import LaunchDescription
from launch_ros.actions import Node


def generate_launch_description():
    return LaunchDescription([
        Node(package='my_robot_pkg', executable='camera_node',
             name='camera', parameters=[{'fps': 30, 'device': '/dev/video0'}]),
        Node(package='my_robot_pkg', executable='perception_node',
             name='perception',
             remappings=[('image_in', '/camera/image_raw')]),
        Node(package='my_robot_pkg', executable='controller_node',
             name='controller', output='screen'),
    ])
ros2 launch my_robot_pkg bringup.launch.py
ros2 launch my_robot_pkg bringup.launch.py fps:=60      # override an argument

Remapping

Remapping renames a node's topics from outside, without touching its source. It's what lets you reuse a package that publishes to /image in a system that expects /camera/image_raw, or run two identical cameras under different names. This is a genuinely important idea — it's the mechanism that makes third-party packages composable in practice rather than only in theory.

Parameters

Parameters are named, typed configuration values a node reads at startup and can expose for change at runtime:

ros2 param list
ros2 param get /camera fps
ros2 param set /camera fps 60
ros2 param dump /camera > camera_params.yaml
# In the node:
self.declare_parameter('fps', 30)
fps = self.get_parameter('fps').get_parameter_value().integer_value

The habit worth forming: anything you might want to change — a rate, a threshold, a device path, an IP address — should be a parameter, not a literal in your source. It costs two lines and it means tuning doesn't require a rebuild, a code review, or a Git commit.

Part III — The Layer Underneath

DDS, QoS & Discovery — Chapter 10

You can use ROS 2 for a long time without thinking about DDS, right up until the day two nodes refuse to talk and everything looks correct. This chapter is that day's insurance.

What DDS is

DDS (Data Distribution Service) is an industry standard for real-time publish–subscribe messaging, used in aerospace, defence, and industrial control long before ROS adopted it. ROS 2 doesn't implement its own networking — it sits on top of a DDS implementation, and rclpy's topics are DDS topics with a ROS naming convention layered on.

The practical consequence is worth stating plainly, because it explains something that otherwise looks strange: a program can speak DDS directly, without ROS 2, and interoperate at the wire level. That is exactly what the Unitree G1's SDK does — it publishes and subscribes to DDS topics like rt/lowstate using CycloneDDS, with no ROS installed anywhere (G1 guide). It's also why a ROS 2 bridge for the G1 is possible at all: both sides were already speaking the same protocol.

Quality of Service

QoS is a set of per-topic policies controlling delivery guarantees, and it is the single most common cause of "the topic exists, both nodes are running, and nothing arrives". A publisher and subscriber whose QoS profiles are incompatible simply do not connect — silently.

PolicyOptionsWhat it means
ReliabilityRELIABLE / BEST_EFFORTRetry until delivered, or send once and accept loss
DurabilityVOLATILE / TRANSIENT_LOCALWhether late subscribers get previously-sent messages
HistoryKEEP_LAST(n) / KEEP_ALLHow much to buffer
Deadline / LivelinessdurationsExpected message interval; how a node proves it's alive

The incompatibility that catches everyone: a RELIABLE subscriber will not connect to a BEST_EFFORT publisher. The subscriber is demanding a guarantee the publisher hasn't offered. The reverse is fine. Sensor drivers commonly publish BEST_EFFORT (for a camera at 30 Hz, the newest frame matters more than a retransmitted old one), while the default for a hand-written subscriber is RELIABLE — so a first attempt to subscribe to a camera very often produces exactly nothing.

ros2 topic info /image_raw --verbose      # shows the QoS on both sides

Read both profiles from that output and compare. It's the diagnostic for this entire class of bug.

Discovery and ROS_DOMAIN_ID

Nodes find each other by multicast on the local network. Two consequences follow, both of which surprise people in a shared lab:

  • Everyone on the subnet shares a graph. Your classmate's /cmd_vel is your /cmd_vel. Set ROS_DOMAIN_ID to partition yourself off — nodes only see nodes with the same ID. Official guidance is to pick a value between 0 and 101 inclusive; on Linux those (and 215–232) avoid colliding with the kernel's ephemeral port range.
  • Discovery needs multicast. Many managed networks, VPNs, and Docker's default bridge block it. Nodes on the same machine work; nodes across machines never find each other.
export ROS_DOMAIN_ID=42                  # pick a number; set it in every terminal
export ROS_LOCALHOST_ONLY=1              # stay on this machine entirely
ros2 doctor                              # general environment check
ros2 daemon stop && ros2 daemon start    # fixes stale CLI listings
From the field — the silent failure is the signature ROS 2's hardest bugs share one characteristic: nothing errors. A QoS mismatch doesn't warn you. A ROS_DOMAIN_ID difference doesn't warn you. A topic name typo creates a brand-new topic quite happily. Blocked multicast produces two systems that each look perfectly healthy in isolation. Every one of these presents identically — as "the data isn't arriving" — with no diagnostic pointing anywhere. So build the checklist reflex, in order: is the node alive, does the topic name match exactly, does --verbose show a publisher and a subscriber that actually found each other, do the QoS profiles match, is ROS_DOMAIN_ID the same in both terminals. Five checks, under a minute, and they cover the overwhelming majority of "it should be working" situations. Working through them mechanically is far faster than reasoning, because there's nothing to reason from — the system is telling you nothing on purpose.

TF2 — Coordinate Frames — Chapter 11

A robot is a collection of things in different places, each measuring the world from its own point of view. TF2 keeps track of where everything is relative to everything else, over time — and it is the piece most people underestimate.

The problem

The camera reports a face 2 metres ahead. The arm needs to reach for it. But "2 metres ahead" is from the camera, which sits on a head that has turned 30°, on a torso that has leaned forward, on a base somewhere in a room. Converting between all those viewpoints — continuously, as everything moves — is a genuinely fiddly problem, and it's the one TF2 exists to solve.

The frame tree

Every coordinate frame gets a name and a parent, forming a tree: map → odom → base_link → torso → head → camera_link. TF2 continuously collects each parent–child transform and can compute the relationship between any two frames by walking the tree — including at a past timestamp, which is what lets you correctly relate a camera frame to where the arm was when that frame was captured.

ros2 run tf2_tools view_frames                     # writes a PDF of the whole tree
ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo base_link camera_link    # live transform between two frames
ros2 topic echo /tf

view_frames is the first thing to run on an unfamiliar robot — the resulting diagram tells you more about how the system is put together than most documentation will.

What goes wrong

  • A disconnected tree — two separate trees with no link between them, so no transform can be computed. Usually a missing publisher.
  • "Lookup would require extrapolation into the future" — you asked for a transform at a timestamp TF2 doesn't have yet. Usually clock skew between machines, or asking too eagerly.
  • Two publishers for one transform — they fight, and the frame jitters between two positions.
  • Wrong frame_id on a message — the data is correct, expressed in a frame it isn't actually in, and everything downstream is quietly wrong in a way that looks like a calibration error.

Visualizing & Recording — Chapter 12

Two tools that change how you work: rviz2 shows you what the robot believes, and ros2 bag lets you record reality and replay it as many times as you need.

rviz2

rviz2 renders the robot's world model — the LIDAR points, the camera image, the frame tree, the planned path, the robot's own body from its URDF. Run rviz2, then add displays for the topics you care about.

The essential distinction, and it's one that takes people a while: rviz2 is not a simulator. It doesn't run physics and it isn't the world. It draws what the robot is publishing — that is, what the robot believes. When the rviz view and reality disagree, that gap is the bug, and it's frequently the fastest way to spot a bad calibration or a wrong frame. (For actual physics simulation you want Gazebo, Isaac Sim, or Lucky Engine — see the Lucky Robots guide.)

ros2 bag

Recording is the most underused tool in robotics:

ros2 bag record -a                         # everything (careful — cameras are huge)
ros2 bag record /scan /joint_states /tf     # selected topics
ros2 bag record -o my_run /scan             # named output folder

ros2 bag info my_run
ros2 bag play my_run
ros2 bag play my_run --rate 0.5             # half speed
ros2 bag play my_run --loop

Because nodes are decoupled (Chapter 2), a replayed bag is indistinguishable from a live robot to anything downstream. Record once, then develop and test your perception or control code against that exact data as many times as you like — no robot, no lab, no battery, and perfectly repeatable.

From the field — record everything, always Hardware time is the scarcest resource in any robotics course or lab. The robot is shared, it needs charging, something is always broken, and the interesting failure happens once at 4pm on the day before the demo. Teams who record bags of every session can debug on a laptop at midnight against the exact data from that failure. Teams who don't have to reproduce it on hardware — which often means it never gets fixed, because the conditions that triggered it are gone. Start ros2 bag record before every hardware session as a matter of routine, keep the bags, and name them meaningfully. Disk is cheap; the third-to-last session with the robot before a deadline is not. This is the same instinct as capturing a working requirements.txt the moment an environment works: the expensive thing is the state you were in, and it is trivially cheap to save it and ruinous to lose it.

Lab Exercises — Chapter 14

All of these run on a laptop with no robot. Do them in order — each builds on the last.

Lab 1 — Inspect a running graph

  1. Start demo_nodes_cpp talker and demo_nodes_py listener in two terminals.
  2. In a third, map the whole system: ros2 node list, ros2 topic list -t, ros2 node info /talker.
  3. Run ros2 topic echo /chatter. Check ros2 topic info /chatter --verbose — how many subscribers now, and why?
  4. Kill the talker. What does ros2 topic list show, and what does hz report?

Lab 2 — Inject and measure

  1. With only the listener running, publish to /chatter from the command line. Confirm the listener receives it.
  2. Publish at 10 Hz with --rate 10 and verify with ros2 topic hz.
  3. Explain why the listener can't tell your messages from the talker's.

Lab 3 — Write both halves

  1. Create a workspace and a Python package. Write a publisher that emits a counter at 5 Hz.
  2. Write a subscriber that logs only even numbers.
  3. Build with --symlink-install, source the overlay, and run both.
  4. Change the rate to 20 Hz and re-run without rebuilding. Why does that work?

Lab 4 — Break QoS on purpose

  1. Write a publisher with BEST_EFFORT reliability and a subscriber with the default RELIABLE.
  2. Run both. Confirm no data arrives and that nothing reports an error.
  3. Diagnose it using only ros2 topic info --verbose.
  4. Fix it, then explain why the opposite pairing works fine.

Lab 5 — Record and replay

  1. Record your Lab 3 topic for 30 seconds into a named bag.
  2. Stop the publisher entirely. Run ros2 bag info and inspect what you captured.
  3. Play the bag back and confirm your subscriber behaves identically.
  4. Play at --rate 0.25. What does this let you do that live hardware doesn't?

Lab 6 — Launch it all

  1. Write a launch file starting both your nodes, with the rate as a parameter.
  2. Launch with a non-default rate from the command line.
  3. Add a remapping so the subscriber listens on a differently-named topic, without editing either node.

Basics and infrastructure

TypeCarries
std_msgs/msg/String, Bool, Float64, Int32Single values. Fine for demos; avoid in real systems — no timestamp, no frame
std_msgs/msg/HeaderTimestamp + frame_id. Embedded in most sensor messages
tf2_msgs/msg/TFMessageWhat flows on /tf and /tf_static

std_srvs/srv/Empty, SetBool, TriggerThe generic services you'll see most often
Prefer the standard type It's tempting to define a custom message for everything, and occasionally you genuinely need one. But every time you use a standard type instead, you get the whole ecosystem for free — rviz2 can display it, existing packages can consume it, ros2 bag handles it, and the next person to read your code already knows what it means. Publishing your robot's joints as sensor_msgs/msg/JointState rather than a bespoke array means every off-the-shelf tool works with your robot immediately. Check ros2 interface list before writing a .msg file; the type you want usually already exists.
A closing word ROS 2's difficulty is almost entirely front-loaded. The concepts are genuinely small — processes that broadcast on named channels, and tools to watch them — but there's a lot of surface area around them, and the failure modes are quiet in a way that punishes guessing. What gets you past it isn't memorizing commands; it's holding the graph in your head and reaching for ros2 topic echo before you reach for a theory. Everything in Part III exists for the day the model isn't enough, and that day comes for everyone. When it does, work the checklist rather than reasoning about it — the system isn't going to tell you anything, so you have to go and look. Then go build something that moves.
