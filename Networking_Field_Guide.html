Part I — How Networks Work

What a Network Is & Why Layers — Chapter 1

A network is just two or more devices that can exchange data — but the moment you ask *how*, you hit a stack of problems that would be unmanageable if solved all at once. The single most important idea in all of networking is that we don't: we split the problem into layers. Get the layer concept and everything else in this book has a place to hang.

The problem networking has to solve

To get a message from your laptop to a server across the world, a staggering number of things must happen: electrical or radio signals on a wire, agreement on how to share that wire, addressing so the right machine gets it, finding a path across dozens of intermediate networks, making sure nothing was lost, and finally handing the data to the right program. Trying to design one system that does all of that is hopeless. So networking does what good engineering always does with overwhelming complexity: it divides the problem into layers, each with one job, each talking only to the layers directly above and below it.

Why layering is the master idea

  • Each layer has one job and trusts the layer below to handle its job. The part that finds a path across the internet doesn't care whether the physical link is fiber or Wi-Fi — that's a lower layer's problem.
  • Layers can change independently. We swapped copper for fiber for wireless without redesigning the web, because the web lives at a higher layer that doesn't care how the bits physically move.
  • Troubleshooting becomes tractable — the single biggest payoff for you. When something breaks, you isolate which layer failed and ignore the rest (Chapter 24). "Is it physical, or addressing, or DNS?" is a far better question than "why is it broken?"

Encapsulation — how the layers actually cooperate

When you send data, each layer wraps it in its own header (and the bottom adds a trailer), like nesting envelopes. Your web request gets an HTTP layer, then a TCP header (port info), then an IP header (addresses), then an Ethernet frame (local delivery) — each envelope addressed to its peer layer on the other side. The receiving machine unwraps them in reverse. This encapsulation is why a packet capture (Chapter 26) shows you nested layers, and why each tool in this book operates at a specific layer.

From the field — "which layer?" is the question that solves networks Every experienced network person troubleshoots the same way: they mentally walk the layers. Cable unplugged or no link light? Physical. Got a link but no IP? Data link / DHCP. Have an IP but can't reach the gateway? Network/local. Reach IPs but not names? DNS (application). The fault is almost always at one layer, and naming that layer eliminates everything else. This is why this book is built bottom-up — not because you'll recite the OSI model in an interview (you might), but because the layer model is the actual mental tool you'll use every time something breaks (Chapter 24). Learn to think in layers and "the network is down" stops being scary and starts being a quick walk down a checklist.

The Models — OSI & TCP/IP — Chapter 2

Two layer models dominate. The OSI model (7 layers) is the teaching and troubleshooting reference; the TCP/IP model (4 layers) is what the internet actually runs. You'll use OSI to *name* where a problem is and TCP/IP to understand how the real stack works. Know both, and know how they map.

Layers 8 and 9 — the field joke that's actually useful

Engineers talk about "Layer 8" (the user) and sometimes "Layer 9" (politics/budget) problems. It's a joke, but it encodes real wisdom: a huge fraction of "network problems" aren't in layers 1–7 at all — they're a user misunderstanding, a wrong expectation, or a policy decision. When the stack checks out clean, look up at Layer 8.

Why this maps to troubleshooting

The whole reason to memorize the layers is the divide-and-conquer method (Chapter 24): start at the bottom and work up (is there link? an IP? a route? DNS? does the app work?), or split in the middle. Each tool you'll learn keys to a layer — ping tests layer 3, nslookup tests DNS at layer 7, link lights test layer 1, Wireshark shows you all of them (Chapter 25). Naming the layer of a symptom is 80% of the diagnosis.

From the field — you'll use OSI as a checklist, not a recitation Beginners memorize the seven layers for an exam and forget them. Working network people use them constantly but informally — as a mental checklist that runs bottom-up when something breaks. "No link light" (1) → "link but no IP" (DHCP, 2-3) → "IP but can't ping the gateway" (3, local) → "ping IPs but not names" (DNS, 7) → "everything pings but the app fails" (8, the user, or the app config). That ladder is the OSI model doing its real job. Internalize the order and direction, and the model stops being trivia and becomes the single most-used troubleshooting tool you own. The TCP/IP model is what's really running; OSI is the language you'll think in.

Physical & Data-Link Layers — Chapter 3

The bottom two layers are where bits become signals and where devices on the same local link find each other. They're unglamorous, but a huge share of real "network problems" live here — a bad cable, a duplex mismatch, a wrong MAC — so understanding them pays off every time something is mysteriously broken.

Layer 1 — Physical: bits on a medium

The physical layer is how a 1 or a 0 actually travels: voltage on copper, light pulses on fiber, radio waves in the air. What a tech actually needs from it:

Physical-layer faults are concrete and common: a damaged or too-long cable, a bad port, the wrong cable type, no link light. Always check layer 1 first — it's the cheapest thing to rule out and it's wrong more often than people admit (Chapter 28).

Layer 2 — Data Link: local delivery on one segment

The data-link layer moves frames between devices on the same local network. Its dominant technology is Ethernet, and its addressing is the MAC address.

  • A MAC address is a 48-bit hardware address burned into each network adapter (e.g. a4:bb:6d:1f:30:9c). The first half identifies the manufacturer (OUI). It's flat (no hierarchy) and only meaningful on the local segment.
  • A frame wraps your packet with a source and destination MAC plus an error-check (FCS). Switches forward frames by MAC (Chapter 4).
  • MAC vs IP — the crucial distinction: the MAC gets a frame across one link (local); the IP gets a packet across many links (end-to-end, Chapter 5). ARP is the glue that maps "I know the IP, what's the MAC?" on the local segment.

ARP — the local IP-to-MAC lookup

When your machine wants to send to an IP on the local network, it needs that device's MAC to build the frame. ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) broadcasts "who has 192.168.1.10?" and the owner replies with its MAC. Your machine caches it (arp -a). This matters for troubleshooting (a stale/wrong ARP entry breaks local connectivity) and security (ARP spoofing enables man-in-the-middle — FTG Ch 11, Red Team Ch 19).

The gotchas that bite techs

  • Duplex mismatch — one side full-duplex, the other half. The link "works" but is dreadfully slow with errors. A classic, maddening layer-1/2 fault — check that both ends auto-negotiated the same.
  • MTU — the max frame payload size (usually 1500 bytes). Mismatches (common with VPNs/tunnels) cause "some sites load, big transfers hang" (Chapter 27).
  • Bad cable / port — intermittent errors, no link, or a link that flaps. Swap to isolate (Chapter 28).
From the field — check the cable, then check duplex The two most underrated faults in all of networking live here. First, the physical cable: techs spend hours debugging "the network" when the cable is crimped wrong, too long, or the port is dead — a $5 cable tester and a glance at the link light would have ended it in seconds (Chapter 28). Second, duplex mismatch: a link that's up but inexplicably slow, dropping packets under load, is the signature — and it baffles people because everything "looks connected." When the lights are green but it's slow or flaky, drop to layer 1 and 2 before you blame anything higher. The bottom of the stack is humble, but it's where a shocking number of outages actually are.

Switching & VLANs — Chapter 4

Switches are the workhorses of the local network — and understanding how they forward frames, what a broadcast domain is, and how VLANs slice one physical switch into many logical networks is foundational to both design and troubleshooting. This is also where network segmentation (and a lot of security) begins.

How a switch actually works

A switch forwards Ethernet frames between its ports based on MAC addresses (Chapter 3). It learns by watching: when a frame arrives, it notes "this source MAC is on this port" in its MAC address table. Then:

  • Known destination → forward the frame out only the one port that MAC lives on (efficient, private).
  • Unknown destination → flood it out all ports (and learn the reply).
  • Broadcast (destination ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff) → send out all ports.

This is why a switch (unlike an old hub) gives each device its own collision-free path — and why you can't passively sniff other devices' traffic on a switch without a port mirror or tap (relevant to Chapter 26 and Red Team Ch 7).

Collision domains vs broadcast domains

Two terms that confuse everyone, made simple: a switch breaks up collision domains (every port is its own), but all its ports are in one broadcast domain — a broadcast from any device reaches every other device on the switch. A router (Chapter 8) is what separates broadcast domains. This matters because too many devices in one broadcast domain = broadcast traffic everyone has to process = a flat, noisy, hard-to-secure network. The fix is VLANs.

VLANs — logical networks on shared hardware

A VLAN (Virtual LAN) lets you carve one physical switch into several independent logical switches. Ports assigned to VLAN 10 behave as one network; ports in VLAN 20 as a totally separate one — even on the same switch. Devices in different VLANs can't talk without a router (or layer-3 switch) between them. Why this is everywhere:

  • Segmentation — separate staff, guests, servers, VoIP, IoT, and security cameras so a compromise in one can't reach the others (the backbone of book two's segmentation).
  • Broadcast control — smaller broadcast domains = less noise.
  • Flexibility — group devices logically regardless of physical location.

Access ports, trunks, and tagging

Two port types: an access port belongs to one VLAN and connects an end device (a PC sees normal untagged traffic). A trunk port carries many VLANs between switches (or to a router), tagging each frame with its VLAN ID using 802.1Q. The tag is how a frame keeps its VLAN identity as it crosses the trunk. "Inter-VLAN routing" (a router-on-a-stick or a layer-3 switch) is what lets VLANs talk to each other when you want them to.

Spanning Tree (STP) — why loops are catastrophic

If you connect switches in a loop (for redundancy), broadcasts circle forever and multiply — a broadcast storm that melts the network in seconds. STP (Spanning Tree Protocol) prevents this by detecting loops and disabling redundant paths until they're needed. You don't usually configure it deeply, but you must know it exists: "the whole network died when someone plugged a cable into two ports" is a loop/STP story, and "a port takes 30 seconds to start working" is often STP converging.

From the field — VLANs are where flat networks go to get secure The single most common design failure in small networks is flatness: everything — PCs, printers, the guest Wi-Fi, the IP cameras, the IoT thermostat — in one big broadcast domain where any device can reach any other. That's how a compromised IoT gadget pivots to the file server (Red Team Ch 21). VLANs are the fix, and they cost nothing but configuration. When you look at a network you're responsible for, ask "what's segmented from what?" — and if the answer is "nothing," that's your highest-value improvement. Just remember the loop lesson too: redundant switch links without STP awareness is how you take the whole thing down with one cable.

IP Addressing & Subnetting — Chapter 5

This is the chapter people fear and the one that unlocks everything. IP addressing and subnetting feel like math until you see the pattern — then they're mechanical. If networking ever felt like fog, it's almost always because subnetting never clicked. Let's make it click.

What an IP address is

An IPv4 address is 32 bits, written as four octets (0–255) separated by dots: 192.168.1.10. Unlike a MAC (flat, local — Chapter 3), an IP is hierarchical: part identifies the network and part identifies the host on it. The split between those two parts is what subnetting is all about, and it's defined by the subnet mask.

The subnet mask and CIDR

The subnet mask marks which bits are "network" (1s) and which are "host" (0s). 255.255.255.0 means the first 24 bits are network, last 8 are host. CIDR notation writes that as /24 — just the count of network bits. So 192.168.1.10/24 means: network bits = 24, this address lives in network 192.168.1.0, and the host part is the last octet.

The three numbers every subnet has

Every subnet has three special addresses, and confusing them is the #1 beginner error:

  • Network address — the first address (all host bits 0). Names the subnet; not usable by a host. E.g. 192.168.1.0/24.
  • Broadcast address — the last address (all host bits 1). Reaches everyone on the subnet; not usable by a host. E.g. 192.168.1.255.
  • Usable host range — everything between. E.g. .1 through .254. (This is why a /24 has 256 addresses but only 254 usable — you lose the network and broadcast.)

The formula: a subnet with n host bits has 2n total addresses and 2n − 2 usable hosts.

Private addresses live behind NAT (Chapter 13) and aren't routable on the internet. Seeing a 169.254.x.x address is an instant diagnosis: the device failed to get an address from DHCP and self-assigned (FTG Ch 10).

From the field — "same subnet or not?" is the question The practical reason subnetting matters every day: two devices can only talk directly if they're on the same subnet; otherwise traffic must go through a router (the default gateway, Chapter 8). So an enormous share of "can't connect" problems come down to a wrong mask putting a device in the wrong subnet, or a device thinking its neighbor is remote when it's local (or vice versa). When you're troubleshooting, the first addressing question is always: "given these IPs and masks, are these two things on the same subnet?" Master the subnetting in Chapter 6 and you can answer that in your head — which is exactly the skill that makes networking stop feeling like fog.

Subnetting by Hand & VLSM — Chapter 6

Chapter 5 gave you the concepts; this chapter makes you fast. There's a mechanical method to subnet in your head using one number — the "magic number" — and once you've drilled it, splitting a network or finding which subnet an address belongs to takes seconds. This is the single highest-leverage skill in the book.

The magic-number method

Forget binary conversion under pressure — use the block size (the "magic number"). For any mask, in the "interesting octet" (the one that isn't 0 or 255), the block size is 256 − (mask value in that octet). Subnets then start at multiples of that block size.

# Example: 192.168.1.0/26  →  mask 255.255.255.192
# interesting octet = 4th (value 192).  Block size = 256 - 192 = 64
# Subnets step by 64 in the last octet:
192.168.1.0/26     hosts .1  - .62     broadcast .63
192.168.1.64/26    hosts .65 - .126    broadcast .127
192.168.1.128/26   hosts .129- .190    broadcast .191
192.168.1.192/26   hosts .193- .254    broadcast .255

That's the whole trick: block size = 256 − mask octet; subnets start at its multiples; the host range is everything between each network and the next-minus-one (which is the broadcast).

"Which subnet is this address in?" — in seconds

Given 192.168.1.100/26: block size 64, so subnets start at 0, 64, 128, 192. 100 falls between 64 and 128, so it's in the 192.168.1.64/26 subnet — network .64, broadcast .127, usable .65–.126. Done in your head. This is exactly the "are these two on the same subnet?" question from Chapter 5: find each address's network, compare.

VLSM — right-sizing subnets

Equal subnets waste addresses when your needs differ. VLSM (Variable-Length Subnet Masking) uses different masks for different needs — subnet your subnets. The rule: allocate largest-first. Need 100 hosts, 50 hosts, 10 hosts, and two point-to-point links? Give the 100-host group a /25 (126 hosts), carve a /26 (62) from the rest for the 50, a /28 (14) for the 10, and /30s (2 hosts each) for the links — each block sized to fit, no waste. Point-to-point router links always use /30 (exactly 2 usable) or /31 (a special 2-host case).

Drill it until it's reflex (Appendix B has tables) Subnetting is a motor skill, not a concept — you learn it by reps, not by re-reading. Use Appendix B's reference tables while you practice, then wean off them. Do ten "which subnet is this address in?" problems a day for a week and it becomes automatic. Free practice generators (subnettingpractice.com and the like) give endless drills. The goal: see /26 and instantly think "block size 64," see an address and instantly place it. That fluency is what separates people who understand networks from people who memorized definitions.
From the field — the magic number beats binary every time Schools teach subnetting via binary conversion, and under exam or outage pressure people fumble it. The block-size method sidesteps all the binary: one subtraction (256 − mask) gives you the block size, and everything else is counting by that number. I've watched the magic-number method turn people who "can't do subnetting" into people who subnet in their head in a week. And it's not academic — you'll use it constantly to answer "is this device in the right subnet?", to plan address space, and to read a routing table (Chapter 8). Of every skill in this book, fluent subnetting has the highest ratio of "small effort to learn" against "constantly useful forever." Drill it.

IPv6 Essentials — Chapter 7

IPv4 ran out of addresses, and IPv6 is the answer the internet is slowly adopting. You don't need to master it like IPv4 yet, but you must recognize it, read it, and understand what's genuinely different — because it's already running on every modern device, often without anyone noticing.

Why IPv6 exists

IPv4's 32 bits give about 4.3 billion addresses — long since exhausted, which is why NAT (Chapter 13) became universal as a workaround. IPv6 uses 128 bits, giving roughly 3.4×1038 addresses — effectively unlimited (enough to give every grain of sand its own block, many times over). The point isn't just "more"; it's enough that every device can have a real, globally-routable address again, no NAT required.

Reading an IPv6 address

Written as eight groups of four hex digits, colon-separated: 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:ff00:0042:8329. Two shortening rules make it bearable:

  • Drop leading zeros in each group: 0db8 → db8, 0042 → 42.
  • Collapse one run of all-zero groups to :: (once per address): the above becomes 2001:db8::ff00:42:8329.

So ::1 is loopback (the IPv4 127.0.0.1), and :: is "all zeros / unspecified." The prefix length works like CIDR: /64 is the standard subnet size (and yes, a single /64 subnet contains more addresses than the entire IPv4 internet).

What's genuinely different

  • No broadcast — IPv6 uses multicast and a protocol called NDP (Neighbor Discovery) instead of ARP for the local IP-to-MAC job (Chapter 3).
  • SLAAC — devices can autoconfigure their own global address from the router's advertised prefix, often without DHCP at all (though DHCPv6 exists).
  • NAT is largely unnecessary — every device can have a real address. Security shifts from "NAT hides me" (which was never real security) to proper firewalling (Chapter 19).
  • Dual-stack — most networks run IPv4 and IPv6 simultaneously during the long transition; devices prefer IPv6 when available.
The gotcha — IPv6 is already on, even when you ignore it Windows, macOS, Linux, and phones all enable IPv6 by default and often prefer it. So "I only configured IPv4" doesn't mean IPv6 isn't running — it usually is, via link-local and SLAAC, sometimes carrying real traffic you're not monitoring. This bites two ways: a firewall rule written only for IPv4 may leave IPv6 wide open (a real security hole — book two, Ch 26), and a connectivity problem can hide in the IPv4/IPv6 preference (an app trying IPv6 first and failing). When troubleshooting modern machines, check both stacks — ipconfig/ip a show both, and ignoring IPv6 because "we don't use it" is how it becomes the unmonitored path.
From the field — learn to read it now, master it as it spreads For most techs today, the working level of IPv6 is: recognize an IPv6 address, read the shorthand, know link-local (fe80::) and loopback (::1) on sight, and remember that it's running alongside IPv4 whether you planned for it or not. You don't need to subnet it in your head yet (the /64 standard means you rarely subnet within a LAN anyway). But the trend is one-directional — mobile and large cloud networks are increasingly IPv6-first — so treat this chapter as the foundation you'll build on, and at minimum stop letting IPv6 be the unconfigured, unmonitored stack on networks you're responsible for.

Routing Fundamentals — Chapter 8

Switching moves frames within a network; routing moves packets *between* networks. Routers are how a packet gets from your subnet to a server across the world, hop by hop. Understanding how a router decides where to send a packet — and what a "default gateway" really is — demystifies most of what happens beyond your own LAN.

What a router does

A router connects different networks and forwards packets between them based on the IP address (layer 3), where a switch forwards frames within one network based on MAC (layer 2, Chapter 4). Routers are also the boundary of broadcast domains. Every router does one core thing repeatedly: look at a packet's destination IP, consult its routing table, and forward the packet out the appropriate interface toward the next hop.

The routing table and the default gateway

A routing table is a list of "to reach network X, send via next-hop Y out interface Z." The router matches a packet's destination against the table using longest-prefix match (the most specific matching route wins). The catch-all entry is the default route (0.0.0.0/0) — "anything I don't have a specific route for, send here." On your PC, that "here" is the default gateway: the router on your subnet that handles everything destined off-LAN.

# see your routing table:
ip route            # Linux
route print         # Windows (or: netstat -rn)
# the default route line points at your gateway, e.g.:
default via 192.168.1.1 dev eth0

The local-vs-remote decision (this is the key insight)

When your PC sends a packet, it makes one decision first: is the destination on my subnet or not? (using its IP + mask — Chapter 6). If local, it ARPs for the destination's MAC and delivers directly (no router). If remote, it sends the packet to the default gateway's MAC and lets the router take it from there. This is why a wrong subnet mask or wrong gateway breaks connectivity in specific ways: wrong mask = the PC misjudges local vs remote; wrong/no gateway = local works but nothing off-LAN does (Chapter 27).

Static vs dynamic routing

  • Static routes — manually configured. Simple, predictable, no overhead. Fine for small/stub networks, but they don't adapt if a path fails and they don't scale.
  • Dynamic routing — routers learn routes from each other via a protocol (Chapter 9), automatically adapting to changes. Essential for any network of real size.
From the field — gateway and mask are the usual culprits When a device "can reach local stuff but not the internet," the diagnosis is almost always one of two things, and you check them in order: (1) is the default gateway set and reachable (can you ping it)? No gateway, or a wrong one, means local works and remote doesn't — exactly that symptom. (2) is the subnet mask right? A wrong mask makes the PC misjudge what's local, so it tries to deliver remote traffic directly (and fails) or sends local traffic to the gateway needlessly. ip route/route print shows you the gateway; ipconfig/ip a shows the mask. Those two values, plus a ping of the gateway, resolve a huge fraction of "no internet" calls (Chapter 27) — and traceroute (Chapter 25) shows you exactly where, hop by hop, a packet stops getting through.

Routing Protocols — Chapter 9

Dynamic routing protocols are how routers tell each other about networks so packets find their way without anyone hand-configuring every path. You won't memorize their internals here, but you should know the families, what each is for, and the one — BGP — that literally holds the internet together.

Interior vs exterior — inside an org vs between them

The bigger split is where a protocol runs:

  • IGPs (Interior Gateway Protocols) route within a single organization's network (an "autonomous system"). This is OSPF, EIGRP, RIP, IS-IS.
  • BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) is the lone exterior protocol — it routes between organizations and ISPs. BGP is what makes the internet one network: every ISP and large network announces "I can reach these address blocks" via BGP, and that web of announcements is how a packet from your house finds a server on another continent.

Convergence — why it matters when things break

Convergence is how quickly all routers agree on the new best paths after a change (a link fails, a route appears). During convergence, packets can be dropped or loop. This is why link-state protocols (fast convergence) dominate large networks, and why "the network was flaky for 30 seconds after a link flapped" is a convergence story. As a tech, you mostly care that convergence exists — transient weirdness right after a topology change is often just the network re-agreeing on paths.

BGP and the internet's fragility

Because BGP runs on trust — networks largely believe each other's "I can reach X" announcements — mistakes and abuse propagate globally. A misconfigured or malicious announcement ("I can reach all of YouTube's addresses!") can hijack or black-hole traffic for huge swaths of the internet, and famous outages have come from exactly this. You don't run BGP as a beginner, but knowing it underpins (and occasionally breaks) the whole internet explains a lot of "half the internet was down today" news, and it's increasingly relevant inside large cloud environments (book two, Ch 22).

From the field — learn OSPF concepts, know BGP exists For a tech or early network engineer, the practical priorities are: understand why dynamic routing exists (so the network adapts without you hand-editing every router), learn OSPF at the concept level (it's the common enterprise IGP and the one labs and exams focus on), and recognize BGP as the internet's connective tissue. You'll configure static routes far more often than you'll touch a routing protocol in a small environment, and that's fine. The deeper protocol internals are a specialization (the CCNP/CCIE path); for now, the win is knowing which protocol family does what, that convergence explains post-change flakiness, and that BGP is why "the internet" behaves as one thing — and occasionally doesn't. Build the lab in Chapter 29 and watch OSPF actually share routes; it makes the abstraction concrete fast.

Part II — Transport & Protocols

TCP, UDP & Ports — Chapter 10

The transport layer is where end-to-end delivery happens and where ports direct traffic to the right program. The choice between TCP and UDP — reliable-but-slower vs fast-but-best-effort — shapes how every application behaves, and the three-way handshake is something you'll see constantly in captures and scans.

Ports — the apartment numbers

An IP gets a packet to the right machine; a port gets it to the right program on that machine (FTG Ch 10's postal analogy). Ports are 16-bit numbers (0–65535) split into ranges: well-known (0–1023, the standard services — Chapter 15), registered (1024–49151), and ephemeral (49152–65535, the temporary source ports your client picks for each connection). A connection is uniquely identified by the 4-tuple: source IP+port and destination IP+port — which is how your browser keeps a dozen connections to the same server straight.

TCP — reliable, ordered, connection-oriented

TCP guarantees delivery: it numbers bytes, acknowledges what arrived, retransmits what didn't, and reorders out-of-sequence data. It also manages flow (don't overwhelm the receiver) and congestion (don't overwhelm the network). You pay for this with overhead and latency, but for anything that must arrive intact — web, email, SSH, file transfer — it's the right choice.

UDP — fast, connectionless, best-effort

UDP just sends datagrams — no handshake, no acknowledgment, no reordering, no guarantee. If a packet is lost, UDP doesn't care; the application deals with it (or doesn't). The trade is speed and low overhead, which suits things where a little loss beats waiting for retransmission: DNS (one quick query/response — Chapter 11), DHCP, VoIP and video (a dropped frame is better than a stutter), online games, and streaming. (QUIC, behind modern HTTP/3, cleverly builds reliability on top of UDP to get the best of both.)

From the field — the handshake tells you what failed Reading the handshake in a capture (Chapter 26) is one of the most useful diagnostic skills there is, because where it fails localizes the problem. That three-way distinction — no answer vs refused vs connected-but-broken — turns "it won't connect" into a precise layer, every time. It's the same logic Nmap uses to map ports, just applied to troubleshooting.

DNS — Chapter 11

DNS turns names into addresses, and it is the single most common cause of "the internet is broken." It's also one of the most satisfying things to understand, because the resolution process is a clear, traceable chain — and "it's always DNS" is a running joke in IT precisely because it so often is.

The job: names to numbers

Humans use names (example.com); the network needs IPs (Chapter 5). DNS (Domain Name System) is the distributed phone book that translates between them. It's hierarchical and delegated — no single server knows everything; each level points to the next.

How a lookup actually resolves

When you type www.example.com, your machine asks its configured resolver (often your router or ISP, or a public one like 8.8.8.8/1.1.1.1), which walks the hierarchy if it doesn't have the answer cached:

  1. Ask a root server → "for .com, go to these TLD servers."
  2. Ask the .com TLD server → "for example.com, go to its authoritative nameservers."
  3. Ask example.com's authoritative server → "www is 93.184.x.x."
  4. The resolver caches the answer (per its TTL) and returns it to you.

This is why the first visit to a new domain is slightly slower (full walk) and repeats are instant (cached). It's also why DNS changes "take time to propagate" — old answers live in caches until their TTL expires.

The classic test (FTG Ch 10): if ping 8.8.8.8 works but ping google.com fails, routing is fine and DNS is broken — you've isolated it to name resolution in one step. Then dig @8.8.8.8 tells you whether it's your resolver (works against 8.8.8.8 but not your default) or the domain itself.

DNS as a security surface DNS is also attacked and abused: DNS spoofing/cache poisoning feeds you a wrong answer to redirect you; DNS tunneling smuggles data in queries to evade egress controls (Red Team Ch 29); and DNS query logs are a goldmine for both defenders (spotting malware C2 by the domains it resolves — book two, Ch 28) and investigators. DNSSEC (signs records to prevent forgery) and DNS-over-HTTPS/TLS (encrypts your queries) are the modern protections. For a tech, the takeaway: DNS isn't just a lookup, it's a control point worth monitoring.
From the field — "it's always DNS" is funny because it's true The joke endures because DNS sits between the user and everything, so when it breaks, the symptom is "the whole internet is down" even though connectivity is perfectly fine. The tells: IPs work but names don't; some sites resolve and others don't (stale cache or a specific record problem); a site works on your phone (cellular DNS) but not your laptop (broken local/router DNS). Your reflexes: ping 8.8.8.8 vs ping a-name to confirm it's DNS, then dig @8.8.8.8 thename to tell whether it's your resolver or the domain, then flushdns for a stale cache. Internalize that two-minute sequence and you'll diagnose the most common "internet outage" there is before anyone finishes describing it.

DHCP — Chapter 12

DHCP is how a device gets its IP configuration automatically the moment it joins a network — address, mask, gateway, DNS, all handed out without anyone typing anything. When it works, it's invisible; when it fails, you get the tell-tale 169.254 address and "no internet." Knowing its four-step dance makes those failures obvious.

What DHCP hands out

Without DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol), every device would need manual IP settings — unmanageable at any scale. DHCP leases a device everything it needs to function on the network:

  • IP address (from a defined pool/scope)
  • Subnet mask (Chapter 5)
  • Default gateway (Chapter 8)
  • DNS servers (Chapter 11)
  • Often more (NTP, domain, WINS, PXE boot info) via DHCP options

The DORA process

A device getting an address performs a four-step exchange, mnemonic DORA (all over UDP, since the client has no IP yet it uses broadcast):

  1. Discover — client broadcasts "any DHCP server out there?"
  2. Offer — server(s) reply "you can have 192.168.1.50 with these settings."
  3. Request — client broadcasts "I'll take that one" (confirming which offer, in case of multiple servers).
  4. Acknowledge — server confirms and records the lease.

The address is a lease with a duration; the client renews at ~50% of the lease time. This is why addresses are stable for a while but can change, and why a device off the network past its lease may get a different IP on return.

Scopes, reservations, and relay

  • Scope — the pool of addresses a server hands out for a subnet, plus its options (gateway, DNS).
  • Reservation — tie a specific MAC to a specific IP, so a printer or server always gets the same address while still using DHCP. The clean middle ground between dynamic and fully static.
  • Exclusions — addresses inside the scope range to not hand out (e.g. ones you assigned statically).
  • DHCP relay (IP helper) — because Discover is a broadcast and routers don't forward broadcasts (Chapter 4), a relay agent forwards DHCP across subnets so one central server can serve many VLANs. Essential in real networks.

The signature failure: APIPA / 169.254.x.x

If a device sends Discover and gets no answer, it can't function — so it self-assigns a useless APIPA address in 169.254.0.0/16 (Chapter 5). Seeing 169.254.x.x is an instant diagnosis: "DHCP failed." The device can talk to other APIPA devices on the same wire but reaches nothing else — no gateway, no DNS. Causes: DHCP server down or out of addresses, a broken relay across subnets, a cable/VLAN problem so Discover never reaches a server, or (occasionally) a rogue DHCP server handing out bad info (a real problem — an unauthorized server, sometimes a misconfigured home router someone plugged in, poisoning the network).

From the field — 169.254 means "ask the DHCP question" When a device has no connectivity, the very first thing I check is its IP: a 169.254.x.x address ends the guessing instantly — it's not a DNS, gateway, or routing problem, it's "this device never got a lease." From there it's a short tree: does anything else on that segment get DHCP (server up or down)? Is this device on the right VLAN/cable (Discover reaching a server at all, Chapter 4)? Is the scope exhausted (out of addresses)? Is there a rogue server (someone's home router handing out 192.168.0.x on the corporate LAN)? ipconfig /release + /renew (Windows) or dhclient (Linux) forces a fresh DORA so you can watch it succeed or fail. That one glance at the address — real lease vs 169.254 — routes the entire diagnosis (Chapter 27).

NAT & Port Forwarding — Chapter 13

NAT is the reason every device in your home can share one public IP, and it's quietly involved in a huge number of connectivity puzzles. Understanding it explains why "incoming" connections are hard, what port forwarding does, and why some apps misbehave behind certain routers.

Why NAT exists

IPv4 ran out of addresses (Chapter 7), so we gave everyone private addresses (Chapter 5) that aren't routable on the internet, and NAT (Network Address Translation) on the router rewrites them to a shared public address on the way out (and back). Your phone, laptop, and TV all share your home's single public IP, and the router keeps everyone's connections straight.

How PAT (the common kind) works

What home/office routers do is technically PAT (Port Address Translation), a.k.a. "NAT overload": many private hosts behind one public IP, distinguished by port. When your laptop (192.168.1.10:51000) connects out, the router rewrites the source to <public-IP>:62000 and records the mapping in its NAT table. The reply comes back to :62000, the router looks up the table, and rewrites it back to your laptop. The 4-tuple (Chapter 10) plus the translation table is how dozens of devices share one address without collisions.

TypeWhat it does
Static NATOne private ↔ one public, fixed (e.g. a server)
Dynamic NATPrivate addresses mapped to a pool of public ones
PAT / overloadMany private behind one public, by port. What your home router does.

The asymmetry: outbound easy, inbound hard

NAT naturally allows outbound connections (it creates the mapping when you initiate) but blocks unsolicited inbound ones — there's no table entry for a connection nobody started, so the router doesn't know which internal host to send it to. This is why you can browse freely but can't host a game server or reach your home PC from outside without extra config. It's also an accidental (not real) security benefit — NAT is not a firewall, but it does hide internal hosts from unsolicited contact.

Port forwarding and friends

  • Port forwarding — manually tell the router "inbound traffic to public port 25565 → send to 192.168.1.50:25565." This is how you host a service (game server, web server, remote access) behind NAT.
  • UPnP — lets apps request port forwards automatically. Convenient, but a security risk (malware can open holes too) — many disable it.
  • DMZ host — forwards all unsolicited inbound to one internal host. Blunt and risky; rarely the right answer.

The problems NAT causes

  • Double NAT — a router behind another router (e.g. your own router behind the ISP's gateway). Breaks port forwarding and some apps; you'll see two layers of private addressing. A real and common cause of "this one app won't connect."
  • Peer-to-peer / VoIP / gaming — protocols that need inbound connections struggle behind NAT, hence workarounds like STUN/TURN and "NAT type" warnings on game consoles.
  • Apps that embed IPs — some protocols put the internal IP inside the payload (classic FTP), which NAT doesn't rewrite, breaking them without an "ALG" helper.
From the field — "can't connect IN" is almost always NAT The mental split that solves a lot of cases: outbound problems are usually DNS/gateway/routing (Chapter 27), but inbound problems — "I can't reach my home server from work," "the game says my NAT is strict," "port forwarding isn't working" — are almost always NAT and its asymmetry. First thing to check on stubborn inbound issues: double NAT (is there a router behind the ISP box? look for two private subnets in the path with tracert). It silently breaks port forwarding because you've forwarded the port on the inner router but the outer one still drops it. Put the ISP box in bridge mode or forward through both layers, and the mysterious failure clears. And remember to tell people the truth: NAT hiding your devices is not security — you still need a firewall (Chapter 19).

HTTP/HTTPS & Application Protocols — Chapter 14

The application layer is where the protocols you actually use live — the web above all, plus the catalog of services that make email, remote access, file transfer, and time sync work. You don't need every detail, but recognizing how each behaves makes both troubleshooting and security reasoning far easier.

HTTP — the language of the web

HTTP is a simple request/response protocol over TCP (port 80): the client sends a request (a method + path + headers), the server sends a response (a status code + headers + body). The methods and codes you should know on sight:

MethodsStatus codes
GET (fetch), POST (submit), PUT/PATCH (update), DELETE2xx success · 3xx redirect · 4xx client error (404 not found, 401/403 auth) · 5xx server error

HTTP is stateless — each request stands alone — so apps use cookies and sessions to remember you between requests (FTG Ch 28, Red Team Ch 14). Reading status codes is half of web troubleshooting: 404 = path wrong, 403 = permissions, 502/504 = the server's upstream/backend is down (book two, Ch 16).

HTTPS — HTTP wrapped in TLS

HTTPS (port 443) is HTTP inside a TLS tunnel (FTG Ch 11) that provides encryption, integrity, and server authentication via certificates. For a network person, two practical consequences: the content is opaque on the wire (a capture shows the TLS handshake and the cert, not the page — Chapter 26), and certificate problems are a common, specific failure class — expired cert, name mismatch, untrusted CA, or wrong system clock all produce browser warnings that are real signals, not nuisances.

NTP — the protocol nobody thinks about until it breaks things

NTP keeps clocks in sync, and accurate time matters more than people expect: TLS certificates fail to validate if the clock is wrong (the "your connection is not secure" that's really a clock problem), Kerberos authentication (book two, Ch 15) breaks with more than a few minutes' skew, and correlating logs across systems (book two, Ch 28) is impossible if their clocks disagree. "Weird auth failures and cert errors started after the battery died" is often an NTP/clock story.

From the field — knowing the protocol's port and behavior localizes the fault The payoff of this catalog is speed: when you know a service runs on a specific port with specific behavior, you test it directly instead of guessing. Email not sending? Check 587/465 to the mail server (telnet or nc to the port confirms reachability) before blaming "the network." Web app throwing 502s? That's the backend, not connectivity. "Secure connection failed" on every site? Check the clock (NTP) before anything else. Pair this with the handshake reading from Chapter 10: first confirm you can reach the port (TCP connects), then confirm the application behaves (right status code, valid cert). Reach-then-behave, keyed to the right port, turns vague "it's broken" into a precise, fast diagnosis.

Part III — Wireless, WAN & the Edge

Wi-Fi & Wireless — Chapter 16

Wi-Fi is where most users actually connect, and it adds a whole layer of problems wired networks don't have: a shared medium, interference, signal strength, and channel planning. Most "slow Wi-Fi" calls aren't the internet at all — they're radio problems, and they're diagnosable once you know what to look at.

The standards, decoded

Wi-Fi standards are 802.11 with letter/number suffixes; the modern naming (Wi-Fi 4/5/6/6E/7) is friendlier:

StandardNameBand(s)Note
802.11nWi-Fi 42.4 & 5 GHzThe old reliable

Bands and channels — where most problems live

  • 2.4 GHz — longer range, penetrates walls better, but crowded (microwaves, Bluetooth, neighbors) and slower. Only three non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11) — everything else overlaps and causes interference. This is the #1 thing people get wrong.
  • 5 GHz — faster, many more non-overlapping channels, far less congestion, but shorter range and worse wall penetration.
  • 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E/7) — lots of clean spectrum, shortest range.

The trade is universal: lower frequency = more range, less speed, more congestion; higher = more speed, less range. "Channel width" (20/40/80/160 MHz) trades throughput for susceptibility to interference — wider is faster but more easily disrupted.

Why Wi-Fi feels slow or flaky

Key distinction: signal strength (RSSI, in dBm — closer to 0 is better; -67 dBm is good, -80 is poor) is a different problem from congestion (the channel is busy) which is different from the internet being slow (the WAN). A Wi-Fi analyzer app shows you signal and which channels are crowded — the fastest way to tell these apart.

How a device joins (and roams)

A client scans for SSIDs, associates with an access point, authenticates (the security handshake), then gets an IP via DHCP (Chapter 12). In a multi-AP network (mesh or enterprise), roaming hands the client between APs as it moves — done badly, this causes drops at the edges of coverage. Security (WPA2/WPA3, the passphrase-is-everything reality) is covered in FTG Ch 11 and attacked in Red Team Ch 26.

From the field — "slow Wi-Fi" is usually radio, not the internet The first fork on any wireless complaint: is this a Wi-Fi problem or an internet problem? Plug a laptop into ethernet — if it's fast wired, the WAN is fine and you're hunting a radio issue (Chapter 24's isolation logic). For radio issues, the two usual culprits are signal (move closer / reposition the AP / check RSSI) and congestion (run a Wi-Fi analyzer, get off the crowded 2.4 GHz channels onto a clean 5 GHz one). The most common single fix in homes is pushing capable devices to 5 GHz and setting 2.4 GHz to channel 1, 6, or 11 instead of an overlapping one. And always rule out "it's not Wi-Fi" first — half of "the Wi-Fi is slow" is actually the ISP, and the ethernet test settles it in thirty seconds.

WAN & Reaching the Internet — Chapter 17

Everything so far has been the LAN — your local network. The WAN is how that LAN reaches the wider world: the connection technologies, the ISP, and the structure of the internet itself. Knowing this layer tells you where "the internet is slow" problems actually live, and which ones you can't fix because they're the ISP's.

LAN vs WAN

A LAN (Local Area Network) is the network you own and control — your switches, your subnets, typically one building. A WAN (Wide Area Network) connects LANs across distances, almost always over infrastructure you don't own (your ISP, telecom carriers). The boundary between them — your router/modem — is the demarcation point, and it's a crucial troubleshooting line: problems on the LAN side are yours to fix; problems past the demarc are the ISP's.

How you physically connect

TechnologyNote
FiberLight over glass; fastest, symmetric, low latency. The gold standard where available.
Cable (DOCSIS)Over TV coax; fast download, slower upload, shared with the neighborhood (slows at peak).
DSLOver phone lines; slower, distance-sensitive. Legacy but still common rurally.
Cellular (4G/5G)Wireless WAN; increasingly viable as primary or failover.
SatelliteRemote areas; traditional GEO has high latency, LEO (Starlink) much less.

The structure of the internet

The internet is a network of networks — thousands of independent networks (Autonomous Systems) interconnecting. There's a rough tier hierarchy: Tier 1 carriers form the global backbone and peer with each other for free; Tier 2 regional providers buy transit from Tier 1 and peer where they can; Tier 3 are the local ISPs you actually buy from. They glue together at Internet Exchange Points (IXPs) and via peering agreements, and the whole mesh is held together by BGP (Chapter 9). This is why a traceroute (Chapter 25) to a distant server shows your packet crossing multiple providers' networks — you're watching the tiers hand it along.

Enterprise WAN — the terms to recognize

  • MPLS — a carrier service that creates private, prioritized paths between an organization's sites. Reliable, expensive, traditional.
  • SD-WAN — the modern approach: intelligently use multiple cheaper links (broadband, cellular, MPLS) with software steering traffic by policy/health. Largely displacing pure MPLS.
  • Leased line — a dedicated, guaranteed-bandwidth circuit between two points.

Bandwidth, the SLA, and "it's slow"

Your connection has an advertised bandwidth (often asymmetric — "300/20" is 300 down, 20 up), and business connections carry an SLA (service-level agreement) guaranteeing uptime/performance. Two practical truths: residential connections are usually contended (shared, so they slow at peak times and "up to" speeds aren't guaranteed), and upload is often the hidden bottleneck (video calls, backups, and hosting suffer on low-upload connections even when download is fine).

From the field — find the demarc, then know what's not yours The most useful WAN skill is knowing where your responsibility ends. When "the internet is slow or down," establish the demarcation point and test on both sides: is the LAN healthy (devices get DHCP, reach the gateway, talk to each other)? Can the router reach the ISP (is the WAN link up, does the modem sync)? A traceroute shows where packets start dying — if it's your first hop, it's you; if it's three hops into the ISP's network, it's them and no amount of router-rebooting fixes it. This matters because half of WAN troubleshooting is correctly concluding "this is the ISP's problem" with evidence (the traceroute, the modem sync stats) so you stop fruitlessly changing things on your side — and can hand the carrier a precise report instead of "it's slow."

VPNs & Tunneling — Chapter 18

A VPN builds an encrypted tunnel across an untrusted network, making two distant points behave as if they're on the same private network. They're everywhere — remote work, connecting offices, securing public Wi-Fi — and understanding the tunnel concept clears up a lot of confusion about what a VPN does and doesn't do.

The core idea: tunneling

Tunneling wraps one network's packets inside another's so they can traverse a network that wouldn't otherwise carry them — and a VPN adds encryption to that wrapper. Your packet (private addresses and all) gets encrypted and placed inside a normal packet addressed to the VPN endpoint; it travels the public internet as opaque ciphertext; the far end decrypts and releases the original onto its network. The result: two endpoints act as if directly connected on a private link, even though the public internet sits between them.

The two main use cases

TypeConnectsUse
Remote-access VPNOne user's device ↔ a networkWork-from-home: your laptop joins the corporate LAN over the internet
Site-to-site VPNNetwork ↔ networkLinking two offices' LANs into one routed network

The protocols

  • WireGuard — modern, fast, simple, small codebase. Increasingly the default for new deployments.
  • IPsec — the long-standing enterprise standard, especially for site-to-site; robust, complex, widely interoperable.
  • OpenVPN — mature, flexible, runs over TLS; very common for remote access.
  • SSL/TLS VPNs — browser-based or client portals using TLS (FTG Ch 11).

Split tunnel vs full tunnel

A key config choice: full tunnel sends all the device's traffic through the VPN (everything, including your web browsing, routes via the corporate network — more control/inspection, more load). Split tunnel sends only traffic destined for the corporate network through the tunnel and lets everything else go direct (faster, less load, but less oversight). This choice explains a lot of behavior — "why is my internet slow on the VPN?" is often full-tunnel routing everything through a distant office; "why can't I reach the internal server?" can be a split tunnel not routing that subnet.

What a VPN does and doesn't do A consumer VPN does not make you anonymous or "secure" in a blanket sense (FTG Ch 11). It encrypts traffic between you and the VPN server and hides it from your local network/ISP — useful on untrusted Wi-Fi — but the VPN provider then sees your traffic (you've moved trust, not eliminated it), websites still track you, and you're still logged into your accounts. For organizations, a remote-access VPN is about reaching internal resources securely, not anonymity. Be precise with users about this: "the VPN encrypts your link to the office" is true; "the VPN makes you anonymous and safe" is marketing.

The troubleshooting gotchas

  • MTU/fragmentation — the tunnel's wrapper shrinks the usable packet size; if MTU isn't adjusted, you get the classic "connects fine, small pages load, big transfers and some sites hang" (Chapter 3, 27). A signature VPN problem.
  • DNS leak / wrong DNS — on the VPN you may need the corporate DNS to resolve internal names; misconfig means "connected but can't find internal hosts."
  • Routing — split-tunnel not pushing the right internal routes = can't reach some subnets.
  • Blocked endpoints — some networks block VPN ports/protocols, so the tunnel won't establish at all.
From the field — "on the VPN" changes the whole network picture When someone reports a problem, always ask "are you on the VPN?" — because the VPN fundamentally changes their routing and DNS. Internal resources that work on-site fail off-VPN; the internet that's fast normally crawls on a full tunnel; internal names won't resolve if VPN DNS isn't set. The signature VPN bug to keep in your pocket is the MTU one: VPN connects, you can ping and load small pages, but large transfers or specific sites hang — that's fragmentation from the tunnel overhead, fixed by lowering the MTU/MSS. It baffles people because "the VPN is connected, so the network's fine," but the tunnel quietly broke packet sizing. Knowing that single pattern saves hours on an otherwise maddening intermittent complaint.

Firewalls & Security Devices — Chapter 19

Firewalls and the other security devices in the traffic path decide what's allowed to flow — and they're a frequent (and frequently invisible) cause of "it won't connect." Understanding where they sit and how they make decisions is essential for both securing a network and troubleshooting one, because a silent firewall drop looks exactly like a dead host.

What a firewall does

A firewall enforces rules about which traffic may pass, based on attributes like source/destination IP, port, and protocol. The two generations:

  • Stateless (packet filter) — judges each packet in isolation against rules. Simple, fast, limited.
  • Stateful — tracks connections, so it remembers that you initiated an outbound request and automatically allows the reply, while blocking unsolicited inbound. This is the standard, and it's why "outbound works, inbound is blocked" is the default posture (much like NAT, Chapter 13 — though a firewall is real security and NAT isn't).
  • Next-Generation Firewall (NGFW) — adds application awareness, user identity, and integrated IPS/inspection — decisions based on what app the traffic is, not just port.

Host vs network firewalls

Two places firewalls live, and both can block you: a host firewall runs on the individual machine (Windows Defender Firewall, Linux iptables/nftables/ufw — book two, Ch 26); a network firewall is a dedicated device/appliance guarding a network boundary. A connection can be blocked at either, which is why "the server's firewall allows it but it still fails" sends you looking for a network firewall in between (and vice versa). Default-deny (block everything, explicitly allow what's needed) is the secure design; the implicit final rule in most firewalls is "deny all."

The other devices in the path

DeviceRole
IDSIntrusion Detection — watches and alerts on suspicious traffic (passive)
IPSIntrusion Prevention — detects and blocks inline (can also block legitimate traffic on a false positive)
ProxyIntermediary for requests — forward proxy (filters/controls user web access), reverse proxy (fronts servers, book two, Ch 16)
Load balancerDistributes traffic across multiple servers for scale/availability
WAFWeb Application Firewall — inspects HTTP for web attacks (Red Team Ch 13)

These (IDS/IPS, segmentation, etc.) are the defensive backbone covered in depth in book two, Part V; here the networking point is simply that they sit in the path and can alter or block traffic.

How firewalls cause troubleshooting headaches

The defining problem: a firewall that silently drops a packet (vs actively rejecting it) is indistinguishable from a dead host or a routing black hole — your SYN goes out and nothing comes back (Chapter 10). This is the "filtered" state in a scan (Red Team Ch 7). A reject (active refusal) at least sends back a RST or ICMP error so you know something said no. So when a connection just hangs with no response, a silent firewall is a prime suspect — and you confirm by testing from somewhere the firewall rules differ.

From the field — check the firewall on BOTH ends and in between "It connects from here but not from there" is the firewall signature, and the trap is checking only one device. A blocked connection can be stopped at the client's host firewall, a network firewall in the path, or the server's host firewall — and people waste hours fixing the wrong one. Method: confirm reachability layer by layer (Chapter 24), and when a TCP connection just hangs (no refusal, no reply) rather than getting refused, suspect a silent firewall drop and test from a different source whose path/rules differ. Also remember the both-stacks trap from Chapter 7: a firewall rule written only for IPv4 can leave IPv6 wide open or, conversely, an IPv6 path unexpectedly blocked. Firewalls are doing their job by being invisible — which is exactly why they're easy to forget when something won't connect.

Part IV — Designing & Running Networks

Network Design & Segmentation — Chapter 20

Good networks aren't accidents — they're designed in layers, with traffic deliberately separated. You don't have to be an architect to benefit from the principles: they explain why enterprise networks look the way they do, and they tell you the single highest-value improvement you can make to almost any small network — segment it.

The hierarchical model

Classic enterprise design uses three tiers, which keeps large networks scalable and troubleshootable:

LayerRole
AccessWhere end devices plug in — the switches users connect to (Chapter 4)
DistributionAggregates access switches, does inter-VLAN routing and policy
CoreThe high-speed backbone tying distribution together — fast, simple, redundant

Smaller networks "collapse" the core and distribution into one layer, but the idea — a clear hierarchy with defined roles — scales down sensibly. The goals it serves: scalability (add access switches without redesigning), redundancy (no single point of failure — with STP/link redundancy, Chapter 4), and manageability.

Segmentation — the security and sanity multiplier

The most important design principle for a security-minded tech: separate traffic by trust and function using VLANs (Chapter 4) and routing/firewall policy between them. A well-segmented network has separate zones for, say: corporate workstations, servers, guest Wi-Fi, VoIP phones, IoT/cameras, and management interfaces — with rules controlling which can talk to which. Why it matters so much:

  • Containment — a compromised IoT camera or guest device can't reach the file server (it stops lateral movement — Red Team Ch 21). This is the practical heart of book two's segmentation.
  • Performance — smaller broadcast domains, less noise (Chapter 4).
  • Policy — you can apply different security/QoS rules per zone.

The DMZ and zero-trust

  • DMZ (demilitarized zone) — a buffer subnet for things that must be reachable from the internet (a public web server) but shouldn't have direct access to the internal LAN. If it's breached, the attacker is stuck in the DMZ, not on your internal network.
  • Zero-trust — the modern direction: stop trusting traffic just because it's "inside" the perimeter. Verify and authorize every request regardless of origin, and microsegment so even internal lateral movement is controlled. A philosophy more than a product (book two, Ch 26), and the answer to the "hard shell, soft interior" problem that flat networks suffer.

Designing for failure

Real designs assume things break: redundant links and devices (with STP managing loops), redundant paths to the internet (dual ISPs, often via SD-WAN — Chapter 17), and no single point of failure on critical paths. There's always a cost/complexity trade — full redundancy everywhere is expensive — so you prioritize redundancy where an outage hurts most.

From the field — if it's flat, segment it; that's the win Walk up to almost any small or mid-size network and the highest-value improvement is the same: it's flat, so segment it. Everything — PCs, the guest Wi-Fi, the printer, the security cameras, the smart TV, the building's HVAC controller — sharing one network where any device can reach any other is how a $30 compromised IoT gadget becomes a path to the domain controller (Red Team Ch 21). VLANs cost nothing but configuration and they're the difference between "one device got owned" and "the whole network got owned." When you assess a network, the first question is "what's separated from what?" — and "nothing" is both the most common answer and the most valuable thing to fix. Start with the obvious wins: guests and IoT off the corporate/server network, management interfaces on their own isolated VLAN.

Home & Small-Office Networks — Chapter 21

This is the network most people actually touch, and the one where a little knowledge makes you immediately useful. The all-in-one "router" hides a switch, a router, a firewall, a Wi-Fi access point, and a DHCP/DNS server in one box — and knowing which function is doing what turns home-network mysteries into quick fixes.

What's actually inside that "router"

A consumer/SOHO "router" is really five devices in one plastic shell, each a concept from earlier chapters:

FunctionWhat it's doing
RouterRoutes between your LAN and the internet; holds the default gateway and default route (Ch 8)
SwitchThe LAN ports on the back — a small switch (Ch 4)
Wi-Fi access pointThe wireless radio(s) (Ch 16)
Firewall + NATBlocks unsolicited inbound, shares one public IP (Ch 13, 19)
DHCP + DNS forwarderHands out addresses, forwards name lookups (Ch 11, 12)

Realizing this is the key to home troubleshooting: "the router is broken" is vague, but "DHCP isn't handing out addresses" or "the Wi-Fi radio is congested but the wired switch is fine" points straight at the function that's actually failing.

Modem vs router (and the double-NAT trap)

The modem (or ONT for fiber) converts the ISP's signal to ethernet — that's the demarc (Chapter 17). The router does everything above. Many ISPs supply a combined modem+router "gateway" — and if you add your own router behind it without putting the ISP box in bridge mode, you get double NAT (Chapter 13): two layers of NAT that break port forwarding and some apps. A hugely common, baffling home issue with a clean fix (bridge the ISP box, or use just one router).

Practical setup & hardening

  • Change the admin password — default router admin creds are public knowledge (and a real attack vector).
  • Use WPA3 (or WPA2) with a long random passphrase — the Wi-Fi's whole security (FTG Ch 11).
  • Separate guest and IoT — even consumer routers now offer a guest network; use it to keep visitors and smart-home junk off your main LAN (the home version of segmentation, Chapter 20).
  • Update the firmware — routers are a prime target and ship with vulnerabilities; keep it patched.
  • Disable WPS and UPnP if you don't need them (Chapter 13) — both are convenience-over-security features.
  • Reservations for fixed devices — give the printer/NAS a DHCP reservation (Chapter 12) so its address is stable.

Extending coverage

When one AP can't cover the space: mesh systems (multiple coordinated APs with seamless roaming — the modern choice), wired access points (best performance, needs cabling), powerline/MoCA (network over electrical/coax wiring), or extenders/repeaters (easy but they halve throughput and add latency — the worst option, avoid if you can). The principle: a wired backhaul to additional APs always beats a wireless repeat.

From the field — name the function, and check double-NAT first The mental unlock for home/SOHO networks is refusing to treat the box as a black "router" — instead ask which of its five functions is misbehaving. No address at all? DHCP. Names don't resolve? DNS forwarder. Wired's fine, wireless is bad? The AP/radio (channel, Chapter 16). Can't host anything inbound? NAT/firewall (Chapter 13). And the very first thing to check on weird inbound/app problems is double NAT — an ISP gateway plus a personal router is so common and so silently destructive that it's worth ruling out before anything else (look for two private subnets with tracert). Naming the failing function turns "my home internet is acting weird" from a shrug into a five-minute fix, and it's exactly the everyday skill that makes you the person everyone calls.

Documentation & IPAM — Chapter 22

An undocumented network is a network you re-learn from scratch every time something breaks — usually at the worst moment. Documentation isn't bureaucracy; it's the difference between a five-minute fix and an hour of archaeology. This short chapter is about the discipline that makes every other chapter's troubleshooting faster.

IPAM — IP Address Management

IPAM is the discipline (and the tools) for tracking your address space: which subnets exist, which addresses are assigned vs free, what's static vs DHCP-leased vs reserved (Chapter 12). At home a spreadsheet suffices; at scale, dedicated IPAM tools (or NetBox, phpIPAM) prevent the chaos of address conflicts, exhausted scopes, and "is .50 in use? nobody knows." The payoff: no duplicate-IP conflicts, no surprise scope exhaustion, and you can answer "what's at 10.0.5.23?" instantly.

The change log earns its keep constantly

The most-used document in practice is the humble change log, because the single best troubleshooting question is "what changed?" (the triage instinct from every book in this series). A network that "suddenly broke" almost always had something changed right before — a config edit, a firmware update, a new device, a cable moved. A change log turns that from a memory game into a lookup. Pair it with config backups and a failed device or a bad change is a quick rollback, not a rebuild-from-memory.

From the field — document for the stranger at 3 a.m. (it's future-you) Write documentation for the person who has to fix this network at 3 a.m. with no context — because that person is usually you, months later, having forgotten everything. The network you understand perfectly today is an opaque mystery in six months, and a total black box to whoever inherits it. The cheapest, highest-return habits: label both ends of every cable, keep a current diagram, maintain a change log, and back up device configs. None are glamorous; all of them save you on the worst day. The flip side of Chapter 24's methodology is that good troubleshooting starts with good documentation — you can't reason about a topology you can't see, and you can't spot "what changed" without a record of what was.

Performance: Latency & QoS — Chapter 23

"The network is slow" is the vaguest complaint there is, because "slow" has several distinct causes that need different fixes. Separating bandwidth from latency from loss — and knowing what QoS can and can't do — turns a vague gripe into a measurable, fixable problem.

The three numbers that aren't the same thing

MetricWhat it isAnalogy
Bandwidth / throughputHow much data per second the link can carryThe width of the pipe
LatencyHow long one packet takes to make the trip (ms)The length of the pipe
JitterVariation in latency packet-to-packetThe unevenness of arrival
Packet loss% of packets that don't arriveDrops/leaks in the pipe

The crucial insight: bandwidth and latency are independent. A fat pipe with high latency (satellite) feels sluggish for interactive things despite big downloads; a thin pipe with low latency feels snappy for web browsing but chokes on large transfers. "Slow" might mean any of these, and the fix differs:

  • Slow downloads/transfers → bandwidth (or loss). Upgrade capacity, or find what's saturating it.
  • Laggy video calls/gaming/SSH → latency and jitter. Bandwidth upgrades won't help; reduce the path/queueing.
  • Choppy voice/video → jitter and loss. QoS and queue management.

Measuring it

ping host                  # latency (round-trip ms) and basic loss
ping -t host  /  ping host # sustained — watch for spikes (jitter) and drops (loss)
mtr host                   # latency + loss PER HOP — finds WHERE it degrades (Ch 25)
iperf3 -c server           # actual throughput between two points you control
# a speed test measures bandwidth to the internet; iperf measures a specific link

mtr is the standout: it shows latency and loss at each hop, so you can see the path is fine for five hops and then loss appears — localizing where the degradation is (often the ISP, Chapter 17).

QoS — prioritizing what matters

Quality of Service manages contention: when a link is full, QoS decides what goes first. It classifies traffic (voice, video, bulk, default) and prioritizes the latency-sensitive stuff (a VoIP call) over things that don't care about a few ms (a file download or backup). QoS doesn't create bandwidth — it allocates it under pressure. Most useful where a link is regularly saturated and some traffic (voice/video) must stay smooth; less relevant on a link that's never full.

Bufferbloat — the sneaky latency killer

A modern, real-world performance villain: oversized buffers in routers/modems hold packets during congestion instead of dropping them, which spikes latency exactly when a link is busy — so your video call stutters the moment someone starts a big upload, even though "bandwidth is fine." The fix is smart queue management (SQM/fq_codel) on the router, not more bandwidth. "Calls get choppy whenever someone's uploading" is the classic bufferbloat signature.

From the field — make "slow" specific before you fix anything Never accept "the network is slow" — measure to turn it into one of the named problems, because the fixes are opposite. ping for latency and loss, a speed test for bandwidth, mtr to find where on the path it degrades. The classic misdiagnosis is throwing bandwidth at a latency problem: a company upgrades to a fatter pipe and the laggy video calls don't improve one bit, because the issue was latency/jitter/bufferbloat all along. Ask what is slow (downloads vs calls vs interactive), measure the relevant metric, and localize it with mtr. And keep bufferbloat in your back pocket — "everything's fine until someone uploads, then calls break" is it, and the fix is queue management, not a bigger plan. Specific beats vague every time (Chapter 24).

Part V — Troubleshooting & Tools

The Troubleshooting Methodology — Chapter 24

This is the chapter the whole book was building toward. A method beats a bag of tricks every time, because the method works on networks you've never seen, with gear you don't know. The core move is the same one from Chapter 1 — think in layers — applied as a disciplined, repeatable process.

The layered approach — your primary weapon

Because networking is layered (Chapter 2), faults live at one layer, and you find it by walking the stack. Two directions:

  • Bottom-up — start at Physical and climb. Link light? IP address? Gateway reachable? DNS resolving? App working? Good when the problem looks like "no connectivity at all."
  • Top-down — start at the application and descend. Good when "this one app fails but everything else works."
  • Divide-and-conquer — jump to the middle (can you ping by IP? by name?) and halve the problem in one test. The fastest when you have a hunch.

The questions before the tools

Same triage instinct as every book in this series — ask before you act:

  1. What exactly is wrong? "Slow" vs "no connectivity" vs "this one site" are different problems. Get specifics and exact errors.
  2. What's the scope? One device, or everyone? One site, or all sites? One app, or all? This single question eliminates whole layers — if everyone's down it's not one PC's config.
  3. What changed? The most powerful question in troubleshooting. New device, config change, firmware update, cable moved, ISP work? (This is why the change log in Chapter 22 matters.)
  4. When did it start, and is it constant or intermittent? Intermittent points at load, heat, roaming, or flapping links — a different hunt than a hard failure.

Scope is the great eliminator

"Is it just you, or everyone?" deserves emphasis because it instantly halves (or more) the problem space. One device affected → it's that device (its config, cable, port, NIC). A whole subnet/VLAN → their switch, gateway, or DHCP scope. Everyone everywhere → core, internet, or DNS. You can often locate the fault to a layer and a region before touching a single tool, just by establishing who's affected.

From the field — the four pings that solve most outages For "I can't get online," one sequence resolves the large majority of cases in under a minute, and it is the layered method made concrete: (1) check the IP — a 169.254 address means DHCP failed (Chapter 12), stop here. (2) ping the gateway — fails = local/switch/cable/mask problem; works = your local network's fine. (3) ping 8.8.8.8 — fails = gateway/routing/WAN (Chapter 8, 17); works = the internet path is up. (4) ping a name — fails while 8.8.8.8 worked = DNS (Chapter 11), the single most common culprit. Each step is one layer; the first failure names the problem and points at the chapter. Memorize this ladder and you'll out-diagnose people who've been doing this for years but never made it systematic.

A handful of command-line tools cover the vast majority of network diagnostics. Each one tests a specific layer, so knowing what each *proves* is half the value — you're not just running commands, you're checking off layers in the methodology from Chapter 24.

Reading ping and traceroute like a pro

  • ping — consistent low times = healthy; rising/erratic times = congestion or a weak link; "Request timed out" = no reply (down, or firewalled — Chapter 19; note many hosts/firewalls drop ping by policy, so "no ping" isn't always "down"). Watch for loss over many pings.
  • traceroute — each line is a hop (a router). It reveals where a packet dies: if hop 1 (your gateway) fails, it's local; if it gets several hops into the ISP then stops, it's the ISP (Chapter 17). A few hops showing * * * but later hops responding is normal (some routers don't reply to the probe) — only a persistent stop matters.
  • mtr — the upgrade: run it for a while and it shows loss% and latency per hop, so intermittent problems and the exact degrading hop become obvious. The single best "where is the slowness/loss?" tool.
Where to find them Windows: ping, tracert, nslookup, ipconfig, netstat, arp, Test-NetConnection (PowerShell — a Swiss-army cmdlet for port/route tests) are all built in. Linux/Mac: ping, ip, ss, dig, traceroute, nc — install mtr, dig (dnsutils/bind-utils), and traceroute if missing. Note Windows uses tracert/ipconfig; Linux uses traceroute/ip — same jobs, slightly different names. Everything here is free and on the box; no install needed for the core set.
From the field — each command checks off a layer; run them in order The mistake beginners make is running tools randomly; the skill is running them in the methodology order (Chapter 24), each one proving or eliminating a layer. ipconfig/ip addr first (do I have valid config?), then the ping ladder (gateway → 8.8.8.8 → name), then mtr if it's a path/slowness issue, then nc/Test-NetConnection to confirm a specific service port. By the time you've walked that, you've localized the problem to a layer and usually a device — without guessing. Master mtr especially: "the connection is slow/flaky somewhere out there" becomes "hop 4, the ISP's router, is dropping 8% of packets," which is both the diagnosis and the evidence you hand the ISP (Chapter 17).

Packet Analysis with Wireshark — Chapter 26

When the command-line tools tell you *that* something's wrong but not *why*, Wireshark shows you the actual packets — ground truth on the wire. It's the microscope of networking, and for a network person it's less about security (the Field Tech and Red Team books cover that angle) and more about seeing exactly what your machine is and isn't saying.

The Field Tech Guide (FTG Ch 29) and Red Team Guide (Red Team Ch 7) cover Wireshark for security and capture positioning; here the focus is using it to troubleshoot, where it shines: confirming whether packets are sent, received, answered, or dropped — the things you can only infer from the CLI.

It maps directly onto the layers

Wireshark decodes every layer of each packet (Chapter 2), so a capture is the OSI model made visible: you see the Ethernet frame (MACs), the IP packet (addresses), the TCP/UDP segment (ports, flags), and the application data — nested exactly as encapsulation built them (Chapter 1). This is the best possible way to internalize the layer model: capture real traffic and watch the envelopes unwrap.

Filtering — the whole skill

A capture is a firehose; filters make it useful. Display filters (the green bar) narrow what's shown:

ip.addr == 192.168.1.50          # traffic to/from one host
tcp.port == 443                  # one port
dns                              # just DNS — watch lookups succeed/fail (Ch 11)
dhcp                             # the DORA exchange (Ch 12)
tcp.flags.syn == 1 && tcp.flags.ack == 0   # connection attempts (the SYN)
tcp.analysis.retransmission      # retransmits = loss / a flaky link
tcp.flags.reset == 1             # RSTs = refused/aborted connections
icmp                             # ping and unreachable messages

Right-click a packet → Follow → TCP Stream reassembles a whole conversation (readable for plaintext protocols; for TLS you see the handshake but encrypted payload — Chapter 14).

DHCP Discover with no OfferNo DHCP server reachable — the 169.254 cause (Ch 12)

Where to find it — capturing in the right place Free from wireshark.org (bundles the Npcap capture driver — install it); preinstalled on Kali. Launch, pick the active interface (the one with a live traffic graph), double-click to start. The catch from Red Team Ch 7 applies: a switch only sends you your own traffic, so to capture another device's packets you need a port mirror/SPAN on the switch, a network tap, or to capture on that device. For most troubleshooting you capture on the affected machine itself, which is exactly what you want. tshark is the CLI version; dumpcap for headless captures you analyze later.
From the field — Wireshark answers "is it me or them?" The CLI tools tell you something's wrong; Wireshark tells you whose fault it is. The killer use: capture on the affected host and watch what it actually sends and receives. Is your machine even sending the request? (If not, it's local — routing, config, or the app.) Is it sending but getting no reply? (Firewall or the far end.) Is it getting a reply that's wrong? (DNS handing back a bad answer, a TLS error.) That distinction — "I'm not sending," "I'm sending but nothing comes back," "I'm getting a bad answer" — localizes the fault definitively in a way no other tool can, because you're looking at the actual packets instead of inferring. Don't reach for it first (the CLI is faster for the common cases), but when the simple tools leave you guessing, the packets never lie.

Diagnosing Common Failures — Chapter 27

The methodology (Chapter 24) and tools (Chapter 25) come together here as playbooks for the failures you'll actually meet. Each is a recognizable pattern with a defined walk to the cause. Learn these and most "the network is broken" calls become routine.

Playbook 1 — "No connectivity at all"

  1. Check the IP (ipconfig/ip a). 169.254.x.x = DHCP failed → Playbook 4. No IP at all → check the cable/link (Chapter 28) and adapter status.
  2. Ping the gateway. Fails → local problem: cable, switch port, wrong VLAN, wrong mask (Chapter 6). Works → local network is fine, continue.
  3. Ping 8.8.8.8. Fails → gateway/routing/WAN (Chapter 8, 17); tracert to see where it dies. Works → the path is up → it's DNS (Playbook 2).

Playbook 2 — "Internet is down but it's really DNS"

  1. Ping by IP vs by name. ping 8.8.8.8 works but ping google.com fails = DNS, confirmed in one step.
  2. Test a specific resolver: dig @8.8.8.8 google.com. Works against 8.8.8.8 but not your default → your configured resolver/router is the problem. Fails even against 8.8.8.8 → something's blocking DNS, or the domain itself.
  3. Fixes: flushdns for stale cache; correct or change the DNS server; check the router's DNS forwarder (Chapter 11).

Playbook 3 — "It's slow"

  1. Make "slow" specific (Chapter 23): downloads, or calls/interactive? Wired or Wi-Fi?
  2. Wi-Fi vs WAN: test on ethernet — fast wired means it's a radio problem (Chapter 16: signal or congestion), not the internet.
  3. Bandwidth vs latency: speed test for throughput; ping/mtr for latency and loss. Don't fix latency with bandwidth.
  4. Localize with mtr — which hop introduces the loss/latency? Yours or the ISP's (Chapter 17)?
  5. Bufferbloat check: does latency spike only when the link is busy? (Chapter 23)

Playbook 4 — "Got a 169.254 address (DHCP failed)"

  1. Is it just this device or everyone? Everyone → DHCP server/scope problem (down, or out of addresses). Just this one → its cable/port/VLAN, so its Discover never reaches a server (Chapter 12).
  2. Force a renew: ipconfig /release + /renew (Windows) / dhclient (Linux) and watch it succeed or fail.
  3. Suspect a rogue DHCP server if devices get wrong addresses (someone plugged a home router into the LAN — Chapter 12).

Playbook 5 — "Intermittent / flaky"

The hardest class, because it's not failing when you look. Strategy: correlate with something. Does it drop under load (bandwidth/bufferbloat)? At a certain time (scheduled job, peak congestion)? In one location (Wi-Fi signal/roaming)? After running a while (heat, a flapping link, Chapter 28)? Run a continuous ping/mtr and wait for the drop, capturing what's happening when it does. Intermittent problems are won by patient observation and correlation, not a single test.

Playbook 6 — "This one app/site won't work, everything else does"

  1. Top-down (Chapter 24): the network's clearly fine (everything else works), so the fault is high — the app, its specific server, or a port/protocol.
  2. Can you reach its port? nc -zv host port / Test-NetConnection. Refused/filtered → the service is down or a firewall blocks it (Chapter 19).
  3. DNS for that name? dig the specific hostname — maybe only its record is broken.
  4. Cert/clock? "Secure connection failed" on one HTTPS site — check the cert and the system clock/NTP (Chapter 14).
From the field — recognize the pattern, run the playbook Experience in networking largely is pattern recognition: you hear the symptom and a playbook starts running before the person finishes talking. "Can't get online" → the ping ladder. "Some sites work, some don't" → DNS or MTU. "Fine until someone uploads" → bufferbloat. "169.254" → DHCP. "Works on my phone not my laptop" → compare their configs/paths. The playbooks above are those patterns written down so you don't have to acquire them the slow way. The meta-skill underneath them all is unchanged from Chapter 24: establish scope, ask what changed, then walk the layers with the right tool at each step. Internalize these six and you've covered the overwhelming majority of what you'll ever be called for.

The Physical Layer in Practice — Chapter 28

It's unglamorous and it's where a shocking share of "network problems" actually live: cables, connectors, ports, and the link lights that tell you the truth before any software does. Layer 1 is the first thing to check and the last thing people suspect — which is exactly backwards.

Copper cabling — what to know

CategorySpeedNote
Cat5e1 GbpsThe common baseline; fine for most
Cat61 Gbps (10G short runs)Better shielding/headroom
Cat6a10 Gbps10G to 100m

The hard rule: 100 meters max for copper Ethernet, including patch cables. Exceed it and you get errors, drops, or no link — a real cause of "the far jack doesn't work." Two wiring standards (T568A/B) exist; both ends matching = a normal straight-through cable; deliberately mismatched = a crossover (mostly obsolete now that ports auto-sense with Auto-MDIX). Stranded cable for patch cords (flexible), solid for in-wall runs.

Fiber — the basics

Single-mode (yellow, long distances, laser) vs multi-mode (aqua/orange, in-building, LED/VCSEL). Fiber is immune to electrical interference and spans far beyond copper's 100m, which is why it's used for backbones and between buildings. Practical gotchas: connector types must match (LC/SC), transceivers (SFP/SFP+) must match the fiber and speed, TX/RX can be reversed (swap the pair if no link), and the ends are easily contaminated — a dirty or bent fiber connector is a classic "no link" that a cleaning fixes.

Read the link lights first

Before any command, look at the port LEDs — they report layer 1 directly:

  • No link light — no physical connection. Cable unplugged/dead, wrong port, port disabled, or device off. Nothing higher will work; stop and fix this.
  • Solid link, no activity — connected but no traffic (could be normal, or a higher-layer problem).
  • Link light at the wrong speed — a gigabit device linking at 100 Mbps often means a bad/too-long cable or a damaged pair (gigabit needs all 4 pairs; damage one and it falls back). A real, sneaky "why is it slow" cause.
  • Flapping link (up/down repeatedly) — marginal cable, failing port, or duplex issue (Chapter 3).

The testing tools

ToolTells you

Cable certifierDoes the cable meet its category spec? (the pro tool for installs)

From the field — check layer 1 first, every time The most expensive habit in troubleshooting is assuming the physical layer is fine and debugging upward for an hour, only to discover the cable was crimped wrong, too long, damaged, or in the wrong port. Look at the link light first. No link = it's physical, full stop, and no amount of ipconfig matters until there's a light. A gigabit link negotiating at 100 Mbps is a damaged-pair tell. An intermittent drop that correlates with someone moving a desk is a marginal cable. The fixes are cheap (a $5 cable, a different port) but only if you suspect layer 1 instead of dismissing it. Pros keep a known-good cable in their bag precisely so they can swap-test the dumbest, most common cause in ten seconds — and it's the right answer far more often than anyone wants to admit. Humble layer, huge payoff.

A mask octet is always one of these (the "magic" values). Block size = 256 − the octet value.

The method, step by step

  1. Find the interesting octet — the one in the mask that's not 0 or 255.
  2. Block size = 256 − (mask value in that octet).
  3. Subnets start at multiples of the block size in that octet (0, block, 2×block, …).
  4. Network address = the multiple at or below the host's value. Broadcast = next multiple − 1. Usable = between them.
  5. Hosts per subnet = 2(host bits) − 2. Subnets from borrowing b bits = 2b.

Worked example to copy

Q: What subnet is 192.168.1.100 /26 in?
   mask 255.255.255.192 → interesting octet = 4th (192)
   block size = 256 - 192 = 64
   multiples: 0, 64, 128, 192
   100 falls between 64 and 128 → network = .64
   network 192.168.1.64   broadcast 192.168.1.127   usable .65 - .126

Q: Split 10.1.1.0/24 into subnets of ~60 hosts each
   need 60 usable → /26 gives 62 usable (host bits 6: 2^6-2=62)
   block size 64 → four subnets: .0, .64, .128, .192

VLSM quick rule

  • Allocate largest-first. Sort needs biggest to smallest; carve each from the remaining space at the smallest mask that fits.
  • Point-to-point links use /30 (2 usable) — or /31 in modern gear (2 usable, no network/broadcast waste).
  • Always round up to the next size: need 60 hosts → /26 (62), not /27 (30).
Memorize the block-size row, drill the rest The whole skill compresses to: know the eight mask values (128/192/224/240/248/252/254/255) and their block sizes, subtract from 256, count by the result. Everything else follows. Keep this page open while you practice (Lab 1, Ch 30); after a few dozen reps you won't need it. Subnetting feels like magic to people who haven't drilled it and is trivial to people who have — the only difference is the reps (Ch 6).

A closing word from the author Networking rewards mental models over memorization. You will never remember every protocol field or every command flag — but if you can picture the layers, reason about where a packet is in its journey, and divide a problem in half at the right layer, you can troubleshoot anything, including gear you've never seen. That's the whole game: not knowing every answer, but knowing how to find it methodically. Build the lab, make routers talk, watch the packets, break things and fix them — and the abstract fog turns into something you can see in your head. Once networking clicks, the other three books in this series get easier too, because the network was underneath all of them the whole time.

Sources & Further Reading — Appendix

Networking is one of the few computing subjects whose ground truth is written down completely and publicly: the protocols are defined in RFCs, and the addresses and port numbers are assigned by IANA. Where this book explains why a subnet has two unusable addresses or why 169.254.x.x means "DHCP failed," the source of record is the relevant RFC below — and much of it is also verifiable at your own prompt with the Chapter 25 toolkit.

Standards are stable; assignments and gear are not The protocol RFCs below are stable references, verified reachable on 27 August 2026. Vendor CLIs, default configurations, and the IANA port registry change over time; the subnetting math and the addressing rules do not. Where a specific device's behaviour differs from this book, that device's own documentation governs — but the RFC governs what the protocol actually requires.
