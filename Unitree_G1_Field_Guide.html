The Unitree G1 Field Guide

The Unitree G1 Field Guide

A Humanoid You Can Actually Program
The G1 is a real humanoid robot with real mass, real torque, and an SDK that will let you command any joint directly the moment you ask it to. That combination is exactly what makes it a good teaching platform and exactly what makes it worth respecting. This book covers the parts you need to work with one competently: how to reach it over the network, how its publish–subscribe interface is actually structured, the difference between asking it to walk and driving its motors yourself, and the safety habits that separate a productive lab session from an expensive one.
A companion in the Field Guide series · pairs with the ROS 2 & Robotics Installation guides · offline, self-contained
This book is interactive Press Ctrl+K to search every heading; tick the sidebar checkboxes to track progress (or press R); use ←/→ to move chapter to chapter; toggle light/dark and text size top-right; hover any command block to copy it. Look for ⌨️ Try it boxes — the read-only ones are safe any time; the ones that move the robot are marked, and they assume the pre-flight checklist in Appendix B. Everything works offline.
This book describes a class of platform, not a fixed spec Unitree ships firmware and SDK updates regularly, and configurations differ between the 23-DoF and 29-DoF variants, between EDU and standard units, and between lab setups. Everything here reflects the SDK2 generation and the common lab configuration, and the concepts — DDS topics, the high/low-level split, joint indexing, the safety model — are stable across versions. The specific numbers are not. Verify IP addresses, joint counts, and joint ordering against your own robot and the documentation shipped with your firmware before you send a command that moves anything. When this guide and your robot disagree, your robot is right.

How to Read This Book — Front Matter

This is a hardware book, which changes how you should read it. In the other guides in this series a mistake costs you an afternoon. Here it can cost a gearbox, or a hand.

The journey, part by part Safety First is not skippable and is deliberately placed before Chapter 1. Part I (The Machine) covers what the G1 physically is and how to reach it over the network — the step that blocks everyone on day one. Part II (Talking to It) is the core: the SDK's DDS interface, the crucial distinction between high-level and low-level control, and how joints are indexed. Part III (Building On It) covers the audio and voice pipeline, reading sensor data, bridging into ROS 2, and why you should prove your code in simulation first. Part IV is troubleshooting, labs, and the pre-flight checklist worth printing.
How to actually use it Read Parts I and II before your first lab session, not during it — hardware time is scarce and shared, and reading while a robot is powered is a poor use of both. Chapters 3 and 5 are the ones to understand properly; everything else you can look up. If you're new to publish–subscribe, read Chapter 3 of the ROS 2 guide first — the G1 uses the same model over raw DDS, and it's much easier to learn the pattern on a laptop than on a humanoid.

Safety First — Read This — Front Matter

The G1 weighs about 35 kg, has actuators that can move faster than you can react, and will execute exactly what you tell it to with no judgement of its own. Everything in this chapter is here because someone learned it the expensive way.

The non-negotiables

  • Know where the E-stop is, and be able to reach it, before you power on. Not "know that there is one" — have a hand near it. This applies to whoever is at the keyboard and whoever is nearest the robot.
  • Suspend the robot on its stand for all low-level work. Feet off the ground means a bad command produces a flailing limb rather than a falling 35 kg machine.
  • Clear the volume it can reach. Arms extend further than people estimate. That includes people, laptops on the floor, and the cable you are about to trip over.
  • Never work alone with a powered robot. One person drives, one person watches with a hand near the stop. This is the standard everywhere it matters and it exists because the driver is looking at a screen.
  • Start slow, start small. First motion of any new script: one joint, a few degrees, low gains. Confirm the sign of the motion is what you expected before you increase anything.
  • Test in simulation first. A control loop with a sign error is obvious in sim and violent on hardware (Chapter 10).
  • Never leave a powered robot unattended, including "just while I fix this import".
The specific way people get hurt It is almost never a dramatic failure. It's a routine one: someone is debugging, changes a gain or a sign, re-runs the script out of habit without thinking about what the robot will do this time, and the arm swings. The robot did exactly what it was told. The habit that prevents this is a deliberate pause before every run — say out loud what you expect the robot to do. If you can't state it in one sentence, don't run it yet. That sounds fussy right up until the first time it stops you, and every lab that has been doing this a while does it. The pre-flight checklist in Appendix B formalizes it.

Damping is your friend

The G1's damping state makes joints compliant — they resist motion gently instead of holding position stiffly, so the robot sags softly rather than fighting or collapsing. It is the safe resting state and the thing to return to whenever you are unsure. Know how to command it from the remote and from code before you need it, because the moment you need it you will not want to be looking it up.

Part I — The Machine

What the G1 Is — Chapter 1

A humanoid robot in a genuinely new category: small enough and cheap enough to sit in a university lab, capable enough to be a serious research platform, and open enough that a student can command its joints directly.

The shape of it

The G1 is a bipedal humanoid about 1.32 m tall standing (it folds to roughly 0.69 m) and around 35 kg with battery. The standard unit has 23 DoF — 6 per leg, 5 per arm, plus waist. The EDU line is configurable from 23 up to 43 DoF, adding waist and wrist joints and optionally dexterous hands; the 29-DoF configuration (7 per arm, 3 waist) is the one most lab humanoid work assumes. Onboard it carries an IMU, a depth camera and LIDAR on most configurations, a microphone array and speaker, and a programmable chest LED. Internally it runs two computers: a locomotion computer that owns balance and walking, and a development computer that is yours.

Why the two-computer split matters

This is the architectural fact that explains most of the SDK. The locomotion computer runs the balance controller — a high-rate loop keeping a fundamentally unstable machine upright. You do not get to interrupt that, and you should not want to. The development computer is where your code runs, and it talks to the locomotion computer over the internal network.

So "controlling the robot" means sending messages to a system that is already busy keeping itself standing. When you use high-level control (Chapter 4) you are asking that controller to walk somewhere. When you use low-level control (Chapter 5) you are partly displacing it — which is why low-level work belongs on a stand.

Where it sits in the field

Unitree G1Quadruped (Go2)Industrial arm
ShapeHumanoid — human spaces and toolsFour legs — very stable on rough groundFixed base, one arm

The humanoid form is chosen not because it's optimal — it usually isn't — but because the world is built for human bodies. Doorknobs, stairs, tools, and workspaces all assume a particular morphology, and a machine with that shape can use them without modifying the world first.

Getting Connected — Chapter 2

Network configuration is where every first session stalls. There is no DHCP server on the robot, so nothing works until you set an address by hand.

The internal network

The G1 has an internal Ethernet switch and every device on it lives on 192.168.123.0/24. Typical assignments:

DeviceAddressNotes
Locomotion computer192.168.123.161Runs the balance controller. Not yours.
Development computer192.168.123.164Onboard, yours to use
LIDAR (Livox Mid-360)192.168.123.20Where fitted
Your laptop192.168.123.99 or similarPick a free address on the subnet

Confirm these against your own unit's documentation — lab setups vary and this is exactly the kind of detail that changes between configurations.

Setting a static address

# Find your wired interface name first
ip link show                                  # e.g. enp2s0, eth0

# Temporary (gone on reboot) — good for a lab session
sudo ip addr add 192.168.123.99/24 dev enp2s0
sudo ip link set enp2s0 up

# Verify
ip addr show enp2s0
ping -c 3 192.168.123.161                     # the locomotion computer

If the ping fails, nothing else in this book will work. Check the cable, check the interface is up, check you're on the right subnet. The Networking Field Guide covers addressing and subnetting if any of that is unfamiliar.

SSH to the onboard computer

ssh unitree@192.168.123.164

Set up a key rather than typing a password repeatedly, and add a host entry so you can type ssh g1. Once that works, VS Code's Remote-SSH will open the robot's filesystem as if it were local, which is the most comfortable way to develop onboard — see the VS Code guide and the Robotics Installation Guide.

The interface name is not optional Nearly every SDK example takes a network interface name as its first argument — python3 example.py enp2s0 — because DDS has to be told which interface to communicate on. Get it wrong and you will see the single most common G1 symptom: the script runs, prints nothing, reports no error, and does nothing. It is bound to an interface with no robot on it, waiting forever for messages that will never arrive. Before blaming the SDK, run ip link show, confirm the exact interface name, and confirm you can ping the robot on it. This one detail accounts for a remarkable share of lost first sessions.

Part II — Talking to It

The SDK & DDS Interface — Chapter 3

The G1 speaks DDS — the same middleware ROS 2 runs on, used directly, without ROS. Once that clicks, the whole SDK stops looking like a vendor API and starts looking like a graph you can inspect.

The model

The robot continuously publishes its state to named topics and subscribes to topics carrying commands. Your program joins that conversation as another participant. There is no connection to open and no session to manage — you announce yourself on the network and start publishing or subscribing, exactly as in the ROS 2 topics chapter.

The topics you'll meet most:

TopicTypeDirectionCarries
rt/lowstateLowState_robot → youEvery joint's position, velocity, torque; IMU; battery
rt/lowcmdLowCmd_you → robotPer-motor commands. Full authority — the dangerous one
rt/arm_sdkLowCmd_you → robotUpper body only, while locomotion keeps balance
rt/sportmodestatestate msgrobot → youHigh-level body state — pose, velocity, gait

Message types come from the unitree_hg package for the G1 (the quadrupeds use unitree_go — a common source of confusion when adapting example code between platforms).

Installing the Python SDK

cd ~
git clone https://github.com/unitreerobotics/unitree_sdk2_python.git
cd unitree_sdk2_python
pip3 install -e .

The dependency that causes trouble is cyclonedds, pinned to 0.10.2. If the install fails complaining about a cyclonedds path, build it yourself and point the SDK at it:

cd ~
git clone https://github.com/eclipse-cyclonedds/cyclonedds -b releases/0.10.x
cd cyclonedds && mkdir build install && cd build
cmake .. -DCMAKE_INSTALL_PREFIX=../install
cmake --build . --target install

export CYCLONEDDS_HOME=~/cyclonedds/install
cd ~/unitree_sdk2_python && pip3 install -e .

Put that export in your shell profile, or you'll rediscover it every new terminal. The Robotics Installation Guide walks through this in order with the surrounding setup.

The shape of every SDK program

from unitree_sdk2py.core.channel import ChannelFactoryInitialize, ChannelSubscriber

# 1. Initialize the channel factory with the interface facing the robot
ChannelFactoryInitialize(0, "enp2s0")

# 2. Subscribe to what you care about, or publish commands
sub = ChannelSubscriber("rt/lowstate", LowState_)
sub.Init(handler, 10)

Every program starts with ChannelFactoryInitialize. The first argument is the DDS domain (0 unless your lab has partitioned things); the second is the interface name from Chapter 2. Get the second argument wrong and you get the silent-nothing failure described there.

Try it — read state, command nothing (safe) 
# Pure read-only. The robot does not move. Run this first, always.
import time
from unitree_sdk2py.core.channel import ChannelFactoryInitialize, ChannelSubscriber
from unitree_sdk2py.idl.unitree_hg.msg.dds_ import LowState_

def on_state(msg):
    imu = msg.imu_state
    print(f"rpy={[round(v, 3) for v in imu.rpy]}  "
          f"joint0 q={msg.motor_state[0].q:+.3f}  "
          f"temp={msg.motor_state[0].temperature}")

ChannelFactoryInitialize(0, "enp2s0")        # <- YOUR interface name
sub = ChannelSubscriber("rt/lowstate", LowState_)
sub.Init(on_state, 10)

while True:
    time.sleep(1.0)
This is the right first program on any robot: subscribe before you publish. If you can see live joint angles changing as you gently move a limb by hand, your network, interface name, SDK install, and DDS domain are all correct — and you've verified all of it without the robot moving under power. If you see nothing, stop here and fix it; nothing downstream will work.
From the field — the graph is inspectable, so inspect it Because the G1 speaks standard DDS rather than a proprietary protocol, it is far more transparent than most vendor hardware, and students consistently under-exploit that. You can subscribe to any topic from a second program without touching the first. You can log rt/lowstate for a whole session and analyse it afterwards on a laptop. You can watch what the official example scripts are actually publishing while they run, which is the fastest way to understand an SDK whose documentation assumes you already do. When something isn't behaving, the productive move is almost never to read the SDK source harder — it's to subscribe to the relevant topic and look at what is genuinely on the wire. The same instinct as ros2 topic echo, and it works here for the same reason: underneath, it's the same protocol.

High-Level Control — Chapter 4

High-level control is asking the robot to do something and letting its own controller work out how. It is the safe way to make the G1 move, and it should be your default.

The clients

The SDK wraps common capabilities in client classes rather than making you construct messages:

ClientDoes
LocoClientLocomotion — stand, sit, damp, set walking velocity, adjust stance height
AudioClientSpeech synthesis, volume, and the chest LED (Chapter 7)
MotionSwitcherClientSwitch or release the active motion-control mode
from unitree_sdk2py.core.channel import ChannelFactoryInitialize
from unitree_sdk2py.g1.loco.g1_loco_client import LocoClient

ChannelFactoryInitialize(0, "enp2s0")
loco = LocoClient()
loco.SetTimeout(10.0)
loco.Init()

loco.Damp()                    # compliant — the safe resting state
loco.StandUp()                 # stand
loco.Move(0.2, 0.0, 0.0)       # vx, vy, vyaw — walk forward slowly
loco.StopMove()                # stop
loco.Damp()                    # back to safe

Velocity commands are continuous

Move() sets a target velocity; it does not travel a distance. The robot keeps walking until you tell it otherwise, which has two implications worth internalizing. First, you must call StopMove() — a script that exits without stopping leaves a walking robot behind. Second, if your program crashes mid-walk, the last command stands. Wrap movement in try/finally so that stopping and damping happen even when something throws:

try:
    loco.Move(0.2, 0.0, 0.0)
    time.sleep(3.0)
finally:
    loco.StopMove()
    loco.Damp()

That pattern is not defensive over-engineering. It is the difference between a bug being an error message and a bug being a robot walking into a wall.

The state machine

The G1 moves through modes — damping, standing, locomotion — and commands are only valid in some of them. Asking it to walk while it's sitting does nothing useful. Follow the sequence: damp → stand → move → stop → damp. Most "the command is being ignored" reports are a mode problem, not a code problem, and checking rt/sportmodestate will tell you which mode the robot actually believes it is in.

Low-Level Control — Chapter 5

Low-level control means commanding individual motors directly. It is where the interesting work is — and it is the part of this book to treat most carefully, because the robot's own safety logic is no longer between you and the hardware.

Stand-mounted only, until you have a reason otherwise Publishing to rt/lowcmd takes authority over the motors. A sign error, a too-high gain, or a command sent while the robot believes it is balancing produces violent motion, and the robot will not stop you. Suspend the G1 on its stand for all low-level work, keep gains low, and move one joint at a time until you have seen the result with your own eyes. This is the chapter where the safety habits in the front matter stop being abstract.

The PD control model

Each motor command carries five fields, and understanding them is understanding low-level control:

FieldMeaning
qTarget joint position, in radians
dqTarget joint velocity
kpPosition gain — stiffness. How hard it pulls toward q
kdVelocity gain — damping. How much it resists motion
tauFeed-forward torque, added directly

The motor computes roughly torque = kp×(q_target − q_actual) + kd×(dq_target − dq_actual) + tau. Two intuitions follow. High kp is stiff — it holds position hard and fights anything that pushes it, including you. High kd is damped — it resists fast motion, which smooths things out. Low kp with moderate kd gives you a compliant limb that can be pushed around: safe, and the right place to start.

Ramp, don't jump

The most common beginner mistake in low-level control is commanding a target far from the joint's current position with a high gain. The motor sees a large error and applies large torque immediately — the limb snaps to the target, and the resulting shock loads the gearbox and can damage it. Real controllers interpolate: read the current position, then move the target there gradually over a second or more.

# Read where the joint IS, then ramp to where you want it
start_q = current_state.motor_state[JOINT].q
target_q = start_q + 0.20            # ~11 degrees — a small move

steps = 200                          # over 2 seconds at 100 Hz
for i in range(steps):
    alpha = (i + 1) / steps          # 0 -> 1
    cmd.motor_cmd[JOINT].q  = start_q + alpha * (target_q - start_q)
    cmd.motor_cmd[JOINT].dq = 0.0
    cmd.motor_cmd[JOINT].kp = 30.0   # start LOW
    cmd.motor_cmd[JOINT].kd = 1.0
    cmd.motor_cmd[JOINT].tau = 0.0
    publisher.Write(cmd)
    time.sleep(0.01)

Always read the current position before your first command, and always start from it. A control loop that begins by commanding a position it hasn't checked is the one that breaks things.

arm_sdk — the safer middle ground

For upper-body work there is a genuinely useful compromise. Publishing to rt/arm_sdk gives you low-level control of the arms and waist while the locomotion controller keeps the legs balancing. You get direct joint control where you want it without taking responsibility for keeping the robot upright.

It uses a distinctive mechanism worth knowing about: motor_cmd[29].q is not a joint — index 29 doesn't correspond to any physical motor. Its q value acts as a weight, blending between the robot's own control and yours. Ramp it from 0 to 1 to take authority smoothly, and ramp it back down to hand control back. Setting it abruptly to 1 hands you full authority instantly, which produces exactly the jump this chapter warns about.

Publish continuously

Low-level control is a loop, not a one-shot command. You publish at a steady rate — commonly a few hundred Hz — and the robot expects that stream to continue. Stop publishing and it will typically fall back to a safe state, which is correct behaviour but can itself be abrupt. Keep your loop timing steady, and never do slow work inside it: a file write or a network call in a control loop causes timing jitter that shows up as visible mechanical judder.

Joints & Indexing — Chapter 6

Joints are addressed by integer index, which means an off-by-one error doesn't produce an exception — it moves the wrong limb. This chapter is about not doing that.

The layout

Indices run roughly from the legs upward: left leg, right leg, waist, left arm, right arm. Both variants share twelve leg joints (six per leg: hip pitch/roll/yaw, knee, ankle pitch/roll). Above that they diverge: the 29-DoF configuration has three waist joints and seven per arm (shoulder pitch/roll/yaw, elbow, wrist roll/pitch/yaw), while the 23-DoF standard unit has a single waist joint and five per arm, dropping the extra wrist axes. Because the legs come first, that difference shifts the index of every joint above them.

Do not hardcode indices from a tutorial Joint indices differ between the 23-DoF and 29-DoF variants, and between firmware generations. Copying an index from a blog post, a paper, or another lab's code is how you command a shoulder while intending an elbow. Get the mapping from the enum shipped with your SDK version, use the named constants rather than raw integers, and verify empirically before you rely on it — the exercise below takes two minutes and removes the whole category of error.
Try it — verify the map yourself (read-only, safe) 
# Robot on its stand, powered, damped. Nothing is commanded.
# Subscribe to rt/lowstate, then move ONE joint by hand and watch which
# index changes. Repeat for every joint you intend to command.

def on_state(msg):
    moved = [(i, m.q) for i, m in enumerate(msg.motor_state) if abs(m.dq) > 0.05]
    if moved:
        print("moving:", [(i, round(q, 3)) for i, q in moved])
Write the mapping down for your robot and keep it with the project. Ten minutes here saves an afternoon and possibly a gearbox — and it makes the joint limits real to you, because you feel where each one stops.

Limits, signs, and units

  • Radians, always. The SDK does not speak degrees. Most large-motion errors are a forgotten math.radians().
  • Every joint has limits, and commanding past them means the motor pushes against a hard stop — drawing current, heating, and stressing the gearbox. Clamp your targets in software.
  • Sign conventions are per-joint and not always intuitive. Verify empirically rather than assuming; positive is not reliably "up" or "forward".
  • Watch temperature in motor_state. A joint fighting a limit or holding a heavy pose heats up, and thermal derating will quietly change the robot's behaviour before anything errors.

Part III — Building On It

Audio, Voice & the LED — Chapter 7

The G1 has a microphone array, a speaker, and a programmable chest LED. This is the fastest path to a robot that visibly does something, which makes it excellent early-project territory.

AudioClient

from unitree_sdk2py.core.channel import ChannelFactoryInitialize
from unitree_sdk2py.g1.audio.g1_audio_client import AudioClient

ChannelFactoryInitialize(0, "enp2s0")
audio = AudioClient()
audio.SetTimeout(10.0)
audio.Init()

audio.SetVolume(60)
audio.TtsMaker("Hello. I am a G1 humanoid robot.", 0)
audio.LedControl(0, 255, 100)         # R, G, B

The LED is genuinely useful beyond decoration: as a state indicator it tells you what your program is doing without looking at a terminal — green while listening, amber while thinking, red on error. On a robot you're standing next to rather than sitting in front of, that is real feedback.

Building a conversation loop

A talking robot is a pipeline, and thinking of it that way is what makes it debuggable: microphone → speech recognition (ASR) → language model → text-to-speech → speaker. Each stage has its own latency and its own failure mode, and the total delay is the sum of all of them — which is why a naive implementation feels sluggish even when every individual piece is fine.

Two things matter for making it feel responsive. Instrument every stage — log the time each takes, so when it feels slow you know which stage to attack rather than guessing. And don't block: run the pipeline asynchronously so the robot can still respond to other events while it's waiting on a model API, or the whole robot freezes mid-sentence. That async structure is also what lets you interrupt a reply when someone starts talking again.

Reading Sensors — Chapter 8

Everything the robot knows about itself arrives on rt/lowstate at high rate. Learning to read it well is most of what debugging a robot consists of.

What's in LowState_

FieldContains
motor_state[i].qActual joint position (radians)
motor_state[i].dqActual joint velocity
motor_state[i].tau_estEstimated torque — what the joint is working against
motor_state[i].temperatureMotor temperature. Watch it during sustained poses
imu_state.rpyRoll, pitch, yaw — body orientation
imu_state.gyroscopeAngular velocity
imu_state.accelerometerLinear acceleration
power_v / power_aBattery voltage and current draw

Commanded is not actual

This is the single most useful diagnostic idea on any robot. You commanded q; the robot reports a different q. That gap — tracking error — tells you something real:

  • Small, steady error — normal. Gravity, friction, finite gains.
  • Large error with high tau_est — the joint is pushing hard and not getting there. Something is in the way, or you've commanded past a limit.
  • Error growing over time with rising temperature — thermal derating. The motor is protecting itself and its available torque is dropping.
  • Actual doesn't move at all — gains too low, wrong index, or you're not actually publishing (Chapter 11).

Log both commanded and actual for every joint you drive. When something behaves oddly, that pair of traces usually contains the answer, and no amount of reading your control code will substitute for it.

Log everything, always

Write rt/lowstate to disk for every hardware session, timestamped. Robot time is expensive and shared; a session you recorded can be analysed all week on a laptop, and the interesting failure — the one that happened once, right before the demo — is only debuggable if you captured it. This is the same argument as ros2 bag, and it applies with more force here because you can't re-run a humanoid as cheaply as a simulation.

Bridging to ROS 2 — Chapter 9

The G1's SDK is not ROS 2 — but because both speak DDS, connecting them is far less work than it would otherwise be.

Why a bridge is even possible

As the ROS 2 DDS chapter explains, ROS 2 topics are DDS topics with a naming convention layered on. The G1 publishes DDS topics directly. So both are already on the same wire protocol, and bridging is a matter of message-definition translation rather than protocol translation. Unitree's unitree_ros2 package does exactly this.

Setting it up

The bridge needs CycloneDDS configured to use the interface facing the robot, which is done through environment variables and an XML config:

export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_cyclonedds_cpp
export CYCLONEDDS_URI=file:///path/to/cyclonedds.xml

# The config names the interface — the same one from Chapter 2
# <CycloneDDS><Domain><General><Interfaces>
#   <NetworkInterface name="enp2s0" priority="default" multicast="default" />
# </Interfaces></General></Domain></CycloneDDS>

source ~/unitree_ros2/setup.sh          # robot-facing interface
source ~/unitree_ros2/setup_local.sh    # loopback, for testing without a robot

Once sourced, the G1's topics appear to ros2 topic list and the entire ROS 2 toolkit applies — echo, hz, bag record, rviz2. That is a substantial practical win: you get mature tooling over a vendor interface for the cost of some configuration.

When to bridge and when not to

Use the raw SDK whenBridge to ROS 2 when
Writing tight control loops — fewer layers, less latencyYou want rviz2, rqt, or ros2 bag
Following Unitree's examples directlyIntegrating existing ROS 2 packages (Nav2, MoveIt, perception)
Minimal dependencies on the onboard computerYour team already works in ROS 2
Learning what's actually on the wireYou need the ecosystem more than the last millisecond

For a first course, the raw SDK is usually the better teacher — it makes the pub/sub model concrete without a framework in the way. Bridge when you want something from the ROS 2 ecosystem specifically.

Simulate Before You Deploy — Chapter 10

Simulation is not a lesser version of hardware work. It is where you find the sign errors, the index mistakes, and the unstable gains — cheaply, repeatably, and without anything breaking.

What sim is genuinely good for

  • Catching the violent bugs safely. A wrong sign is a funny video in sim and a broken gearbox on hardware.
  • Iterating without booking the robot. The G1 is shared, needs charging, and is often unavailable. Your laptop is not.
  • Perfect repeatability. Same initial conditions every run, which hardware can never give you.
  • Generating training data at scale — thousands of episodes for learned policies, far beyond what you could collect physically.

The options

ToolNotes
MuJoCoFast, accurate contact physics. The standard for humanoid research. G1 models are available
Lucky EngineMuJoCo physics with a game-engine editor and rendering; supports the G1 (Lucky Robots guide)
Isaac SimGPU-accelerated, photorealistic. Heavy; needs a serious NVIDIA GPU
GazeboThe long-standing ROS-native simulator. Well integrated, less accurate contact

The reality gap

Sim is not reality and pretending otherwise causes its own failures. Real actuators have backlash, friction, and delay; real sensors have noise and latency; real contact is messier than any model. A controller tuned to perfection in simulation will typically need retuning on hardware, and a policy trained only on clean simulated data often fails on the real robot. This is the sim-to-real gap, and the standard mitigations are domain randomization (train across randomized physics so the policy can't overfit to one exact model) and system identification (measure the real robot's parameters and feed them back into the model). Neither closes the gap entirely. Treat simulation as the place you eliminate the bugs that would hurt, not as proof that something works.

Part IV — Practice & Reference

Troubleshooting — Chapter 11

Robot problems are usually network problems, mode problems, or index problems — in that order of frequency.

Work outward from the wire

ip link show                          # 1. what interfaces exist?
ping -c 3 192.168.123.161             # 2. can I reach the robot at all?
ssh unitree@192.168.123.164           # 3. can I get onboard?
# 4. run the read-only subscriber from Chapter 3 — is state arriving?

Do not debug your control code until step 4 produces live data. Almost everything that looks like an SDK problem is a failure at step 1 or 2.

Lab Exercises — Chapter 12

In order. Labs 1–3 don't move the robot at all; do not skip ahead to the ones that do.

Lab 1 — Connect and observe (no motion)

  1. Set a static IP, confirm you can ping the locomotion computer, and SSH to the development computer.
  2. Run the read-only subscriber from Chapter 3. Confirm live state arrives.
  3. Gently move one arm by hand (robot damped, on its stand). Watch q change in your output.
  4. Print the IMU's roll/pitch/yaw and tilt the robot slightly. Does the sign match your expectation?

Lab 2 — Map the joints (no motion under power)

  1. Using the technique in Chapter 6, move each joint by hand and record which index responds.
  2. Produce a table of index → joint name → observed range for your robot. Note the sign convention for each.
  3. Compare against the SDK's enum. Where they differ, work out why.
  4. Commit this table to your project repo. It's reference data your whole team needs.

Lab 3 — Log a session

  1. Write a logger that records all joint positions, velocities, torques, and temperatures with timestamps.
  2. Record two minutes while a classmate poses the robot by hand.
  3. Plot one joint's position and estimated torque over time with matplotlib.
  4. Identify from the plot alone when the joint was being pushed against.

Lab 4 — Speak and signal

  1. Use AudioClient to make the robot say your name.
  2. Set the chest LED to three different colours in sequence.
  3. Combine them: LED green while listening, amber while processing, back to green when done.
  4. Measure the latency of the TTS call and record it. Where does the time actually go?

Lab 5 — One joint, carefully (robot on stand, supervised)

  1. Complete the pre-flight checklist. Robot suspended, E-stop in reach, second person watching.
  2. Read the current position of one arm joint. Ramp it 0.1 rad over two seconds with kp=20, kd=1.
  3. Log commanded vs actual. Plot both. Explain the tracking error.
  4. Repeat with kp=60. What changed, and what does that tell you about stiffness?
  5. Return to damping. Confirm the joint is compliant again by hand.

Lab 6 — Simulate first

  1. Load a G1 model in MuJoCo or Lucky Engine (Lucky Robots guide).
  2. Port your Lab 5 controller to run against the simulated robot.
  3. Deliberately flip the sign of your target. Watch what happens in sim — and note what that would have done on hardware.
  4. Compare simulated tracking error with the hardware trace from Lab 5. Where does the gap come from?

Pre-Flight Checklist — Appendix B

Print this. Run it before every session that moves the robot. It takes ninety seconds and it is the cheapest insurance in the lab.

Before power-on

  • ☐ Robot is suspended on its stand (mandatory for any low-level work)
  • ☐ The full volume the arms and legs can reach is clear — people, laptops, cables
  • ☐ E-stop located and physically within reach of the person watching
  • ☐ Two people present — one driving, one watching
  • ☐ Battery charge is adequate for the session
  • ☐ Nothing is resting on the robot

Before the first command

  • ☐ Static IP set; ping to the robot succeeds
  • ☐ Interface name in your script matches ip link show exactly
  • ☐ Read-only subscriber run first — live state is arriving
  • ☐ Joint indices verified against this robot, not a tutorial
  • ☐ Targets clamped to joint limits in software
  • ☐ Gains start low (kp ~20–30), first motion is small
  • ☐ Control loop reads current position before commanding
  • ☐ try/finally guarantees stop and damp on any exception
  • ☐ Logging is running

Before every single run

  • ☐ Say out loud what you expect the robot to do. If you can't, don't run it
  • ☐ The watcher knows a run is starting and has a hand near the stop
  • ☐ You know what changed since the last run

Ending a session

  • ☐ Robot returned to damping, then powered down properly
  • ☐ Logs saved and named meaningfully
  • ☐ Anything that behaved unexpectedly written down while you still remember it
  • ☐ Battery on charge
From the field — the habits that separate good labs from expensive ones Everything in this book reduces to four habits, and they are worth more than any amount of SDK knowledge. Subscribe before you publish — prove you can read the robot before you try to move it, because that single step validates your network, interface, install, and domain at zero risk. Read the current position before you command one — the controller that assumes where a joint is, is the one that breaks a gearbox. Log every session — hardware time is the scarce resource and unrecorded failures are unfixable failures. And return to damping when you're unsure — not as a last resort, but as the default state you leave the robot in whenever you stop to think. None of these is clever, and none is optional. The labs where robots stay working for years are not staffed by more careful people than yours; they just do these four things without deciding to each time.
A closing word The G1 is an unusual teaching platform because the gap between "reading about robotics" and "commanding a humanoid's shoulder joint" is about forty lines of Python. That accessibility is genuinely new, and it's why this class of machine is showing up in undergraduate labs at all. It also means the responsibility arrives much earlier than it used to — you will have real authority over real hardware in your first week, before you have the instincts that normally accompany it. That's what the safety chapter and the checklist are for: they're borrowed instincts, until yours arrive. Work through Parts I and II properly, keep the robot on its stand longer than feels necessary, and log everything. Then go build something that walks.
